Where the error comes from

Not the receiver, not the antenna, not the distance to the CORS — and, once the baseline is long enough, not the total station's arcsecond class either.

Azimuth error is σ = √2 · σperp / D. Every millimetre of error at your two marks gets divided by D, the distance between them. That single number dominates everything else, and it is free to change — so make the baseline as long as the parcel allows.

The floor is the carpentry: a normal 10 mm masonry tolerance over 10.3 m is itself 3.34′ of angle. Anything at or below ~1′ is already invisible in the finished house. So 15 m of baseline is enough, 25 m is comfortable, and 5 m is not. The two marks do not have to sit inside the footprint — they only have to be intervisible for the total station's backsight.

The one-receiver column was estimated for a 7 km base. This site is 14.46 km from Telkom Purwodadi, so read that column as optimistic. The two-receiver column is the direct A→B baseline, with both boards logging at once: the CORS never enters it, so its distance does not matter. That is the case for buying the second board.

The whole chain, link by link

The GNSS hands the total station one number, the azimuth of A→B. From there the azimuth has to survive three more hand-offs before it is a string line on a bouwplank. Here is where the error goes, with 25 m between the marks, two boards, a 5″ instrument dropped onto the GNSS tribrach, and the line marked on the boards by crosshair:

The baseline is the weakest link, which is where it should be: it is the one you can buy down by lengthening the line. What can quietly overtake it is not the instrument class. Hold a prism pole on its bubble at M0 and M1 instead of marking the boards, and the marking row alone becomes 85″. Re-centre over the nails instead of reusing the GNSS tribrachs, and the orientation row triples. Choosing the total station works through both.

Absolute position barely matters. Sensitivity of the qibla bearing to position error here is 2.1×10−6 degrees per metre. A 5 m absolute error moves the bearing by 0.00001°. What the GNSS must deliver is the relative vector A→B to millimetres; the absolute fix only needs to be good to about a metre. The header azimuth is for the chosen site itself, one of the 38 points on 110.9274° E whose qibla is a whole number of arcseconds. It still reads 294°15′18″ on a 1″ display anywhere within about 65 m of that point. Recompute it from your real mark anyway; it costs one command.

Before you buy: choosing the parcel

Four things decide whether a parcel suits this house. Only one of them is on the certificate.

Its qibla is a whole number of arcseconds

IntegerArcsecondQiblaDirection found 38 points on 110.9274° E, between 6.94482° and 6.98828° S and about 130 m apart, whose qibla is exactly a whole number of arcseconds. This guide uses the one at 6.965958° S, where the qibla is 294°15′18″. Here is precisely what that does, and does not, do.

  • It is exact at the point. setout.py reads this one as 294°15′18.00000″. That agreement needed a fix: the tool had been using a Kaaba 4.4 m south of the one in the search and the Qur'an app, which put the site at 294°15′17.86″. The whole-arcsecond reading survives about 65 m from the point in any direction.
  • The keypad takes whole arcseconds, so every dialled angle is rounded by at most 0.5″ (0.29″ 1σ). The integer site removes that rounding from the qibla and from every angle derived from the central line alone: the turn to true north is exactly 65°44′42″. The backsight's azimuth is never a whole number, so exactly one rounding of up to 0.5″ always remains, whether you zero on B or set HZ.
  • Its size. Half an arcsecond is 0.025 mm over the 10.3 m line, and one sixtieth of the GNSS term. It is not an accuracy gain. What it buys is auditability: the display reads exactly the published number, a second person can check it without arithmetic, and no fractional second is ever transcribed.
  • What it costs to keep. Nothing: across the 38 points the prayer times differ by 6 s or less, so choosing among them for sky and size is free. Do not trade parcel length for it: ten more metres of baseline, from 15 to 25 m, buy 19″.

Its western skyline

  • Choose by skyline, not by height above sea level. praytime.js v3.2 has no elevation input; height only changes refraction by about 1% per 100 m, roughly 1.5 s. What moves sunset is the skyline: a 10 m tree 5.7 km away already stands 0.1° high, which is 26 s. The Qur'an project's PrayTime.java differs here: it adds a sea-horizon dip of 0.0347√h from sea level, which at 60 m is 0.27°, about 71 s later than v3.2. That is right over a sea horizon, not inland.
  • Criteria by direction. West 280°–294° flat and measurable (Maghrib). West 246°–303° clear above 30° altitude (Asr). The eastern sky dark (Fajr) and the western sky dark (Isha).
  • Rank the 38 points by skyline. Scan them with a DEM (DEMNAS, 8 m) the same way IntegerArcsecondQiblaDirection scans azimuths, and take the one with the lowest western skyline.

Its length

The parcel sets the longest GNSS baseline you can observe, and the baseline is the weakest link in the whole chain. A diagonal of 25 m or more is comfortable; under 15 m the house's azimuth is at the mercy of the receiver. The length also decides which total station is worth renting: Choosing the total station has the table.

Whether the turned house fits

A footprint turned onto 294°15′18″ sits 24.26° off a parcel whose sides run east–west and north–south. Its 6.2 × 10.53 m outer faces then need a 12.15 × 9.98 m box, the long side east–west. A ring of bouwplank 1.5 m out needs 16.1 × 13.9 m. A 10 m-wide parcel whose long side runs north–south cannot take the house at all. Leave room near the west boundary for the gnomon post and its 2.07 × 2.19 m pad, with the view toward 246°–303° clear above 30°.

The receiver: a PX1125R board and an external antenna

A 12 × 16 mm multi-band RTK module on an evaluation board, with the antenna as a separate part you choose.

SkyTraq specifies it at 1 cm + 1 ppm in RTK. It emits raw measurements at up to 25 Hz, as RTCM 3.x or SkyTraq raw binary. In base mode it outputs RTCM 3.3 message 1005 and MSM4/MSM7 for GPS (1074/1077), Galileo (1094/1097), QZSS (1114/1117) and BeiDou (1124/1127). The MSM7 set is the whole of this guide's GNSS data path.

  • The antenna is the part that matters. The module needs an active L1/L5 antenna, biased at 3.3 V, with up to 40 dB of gain and a noise figure under 2 dB. Choose one with a 5/8″ mount, a marked antenna reference point (ARP) and a north mark. A survey-type antenna may even have an IGS calibration. For this job it does not need one: the same antenna model on both marks, turned the same way, has the same phase-centre offset, and it cancels out of the A→B vector.
  • A bare patch needs a ground plane. Use the size its maker specifies. Failing that, a flat metal disc of at least 10–15 cm, and bigger helps at L5's longer wavelength. A housed survey antenna brings its own.
  • Something has to record the stream. The board streams over its USB serial port; a laptop or a small single-board computer running RTKLIB's str2str writes it to a file (Logging). The module itself draws about 100 mA. A USB power bank runs the board and antenna all day; the laptop's battery is the real limit.

Per mark: the board, the antenna and its cable, a tripod, a tribrach with an optical plummet, and a tribrach adapter with a 5/8″ stud. Two marks, two sets — about $120 for the two boards.

Why not a phone. A phone's antenna sits at an unpublished point inside the handset and was designed for cellular coexistence, not phase. Its receiver also duty-cycles unless told not to, and that fragments the carrier phase. The failure that follows is not missing calibration: it is ambiguities that do not fix, and a float solution is decimetres. The board has none of those problems: continuous tracking, a real ARP, a mount that forced-centres, and a choice of antenna.

The stages

Numbered because the order is a real dependency chain, not decoration: no azimuth without a processed baseline, no baseline without both RINEX files.

At the desk · days before

Check the base station before you buy or drive anywhere

cd dsygv/setout
python3 setout.py --cors -6.96596,110.9274     # rank the Ina-CORS stations from the site

Telkom Purwodadi is 14.46 km away, bearing 186°. Everything else is 50 km or more. Download one day of its RINEX and read the header. This five-minute check decides whether the whole GNSS half works.

# open the OBS file and find this block
SYS / # / OBS TYPES   G   8 C1C L1C S1C C2W L2W S2W C5Q L5Q
SYS / # / OBS TYPES   E   6 C1C L1C S1C C5Q L5Q
SYS / # / OBS TYPES   C   6 C2I L2I S2I C5P L5P

You need L1 + at least one more band that your board also sees. The network's receivers are understood to be Leica GR50s, which track a superset of the PX1125R's signals, so the hardware is compatible. What is not guaranteed is that the station's logging profile writes L5/E5a/B2a into the file.

If L5 and E5a are absent, the A-to-CORS run falls back to L1/E1/B1I only. At 14.5 km a one-hour static session is still workable. It hardly matters with two boards anyway, because the azimuth comes from the 25 m A→B baseline, which the CORS does not enter.

Also confirm the station shows green in the SRGI catalogue for the day you plan to observe. Red means the station was down, and the fallback is not “use the next one” — the next one is 50 km away. The fallback is to come back tomorrow.

1
On site · morning

Set the two marks, A and B

  • A is the instrument station, B is the backsight. Put A on the line where the house's central line will run, about 4 m behind the back wall. Stage 5 then pins the house to A. One dialled angle then sets the whole central line, and the EDM's error falls along the line, where it cannot turn it. A also stays outside the trench.
  • Put B as far from A as the parcel allows — 25 m or more if you can get it, 15 m minimum. The far corner of the parcel is the usual answer. A road verge outside the boundary is better still, if you are allowed to use it.
  • They must be intervisible at instrument height, and A must see all four corners (or you will have to traverse, which throws away accuracy you just paid for).
  • Drive proper marks — a patok with a nail, or better, a short length of rebar in concrete. They have to survive until the total station arrives.
  • Keep both clear of walls, parked vehicles, metal fences and overhead wire. Multipath is the largest error you can actually control.
Forced centring is worth more than any receiver or instrument upgrade. Set a tripod and tribrach over each mark, level with the optical plummet, and put the GNSS antenna on the tribrach. When the total station arrives, lift the antenna off A and drop the instrument onto the same tribrach. Lift B's antenna off and drop a prism, on its own tribrach adapter, into B's tribrach. What remains of the centring error is the tribrach's seating repeatability, a few tenths of a millimetre. Re-centring over the nails with a plummet leaves a millimetre or so at each end instead. At 25 m that makes the orientation row of the budget three times worse, which is more than a 1″ instrument buys back. So book the total station for the same day and leave the tripods alone.
2
On site · 1–2 hours

Observe

Two boards ($120 total), the better way: one on A and one on B, logging simultaneously for one hour. You then process the direct A→B baseline, where ionosphere, troposphere, orbit error and base-coordinate error all cancel identically and ambiguities fix in seconds. The CORS is then needed only for A's absolute position, and only to about a metre.

One board, the fallback: one hour on A, then one hour on B, each processed against Purwodadi. The atmosphere decorrelates over the gap and over 14.5 km, which is why the one-receiver column above is worse, and optimistic here.

  • Configure each board to emit RTCM3 MSM7 at 1 Hz and record it with str2str: Logging has the settings.
  • Point the antenna's north mark north at both setups. Same antenna model, same orientation means phase-centre error cancels in the relative vector.
  • Measure and write down the antenna height at each mark, to the millimetre, to the ARP. It barely touches the azimuth, but it is the record's vertical.
  • Check that L5/E5a/B2a are being tracked above about 37 dB-Hz, the datasheet's RTK guideline, before you commit the hour.
  • Do not touch the tripod mid-session. If you bump it, restart the session.
Log RTCM3 MSM7, not SkyTraq binary. RTKLIB decodes RTCM3 MSM natively, and MSM7 carries full-precision phase. RTKLIB's built-in SkyTraq decoder targets the old S1315F raw format, not the Phoenix engine in this module, so the binary path may simply produce nothing. SkyTraq's own Raw Measurement to RINEX Converter, bundled with the EVB, is the other safe route.
3
At the desk · h+2 or later

Pull the Ina-CORS RINEX

  1. Log in at srgi.big.go.id → Unduh Data RINEX. Free.
  2. Select Telkom Purwodadi and the date and hours you observed.
  3. Download the observation and navigation files. You need the navigation file in every case: the board's RTCM stream carries no broadcast ephemeris.

Take the base coordinate from the published SRGI station sheet, not from the file's APPROX POSITION XYZ — that header line is an autonomous fix and can be metres out. Do copy the base antenna delta H/E/N out of the header, though: Ina-CORS publishes real values there and they are not zero. Ina-CORS has the service details.

4
At the desk

Convert, then process in RTKLIB-EX

convbin -r rtcm3 -tr 2026/10/04 08:00:00 -v 3.04 -od -os -o A.obs A.rtcm3
convbin -r rtcm3 -tr 2026/10/04 08:00:00 -v 3.04 -od -os -o B.obs B.rtcm3
rnx2rtkp -k run1.conf -ti 30 -o A.pos A.obs cors.obs cors.nav   # A against Purwodadi
rnx2rtkp -k run2.conf        -o B.pos B.obs A.obs    cors.nav   # B against A (two boards)

-tr is not optional. An MSM message carries only the time of week. Without the approximate date, convbin guesses the week, and a wrong guess leaves epochs that will never pair with the base. RTKLIB settings has both config files. The ones that matter most:

pos1-posmode   = static       # not kinematic; nothing moved
pos1-frequency = l1+l2+l5     # L5/E5a/B2a land in a slot, whatever this build calls it
pos1-navsys    = 57           # GPS 1 + Galileo 8 + QZSS 16 + BeiDou 32; NOT 13
pos2-armode    = continuous   # not fix-and-hold -- see below
ant2-postype   = llh          # NOT rinexhead
No GLONASS, on purpose. The standard PX1125R does not track it, and if yours is the -01 variant that does, leave it out anyway. GLONASS inter-channel bias between mismatched receivers is the most common reason a CORS-based RTKLIB solution refuses to fix. Leaving it out also settles the AR mode. fix-and-hold exists mainly to calibrate that bias; with no bias to calibrate, there is no reason to accept its habit of locking onto a wrong fix. Use continuous.

Accept nothing but a FIXED solution for B against A, and for A against the CORS if you went with one board. A float solution is decimetres and will quietly wreck the azimuth. If it will not fix: lengthen the session, raise the elevation mask, or re-observe away from whatever is reflecting.

6
On site · total station day

Stake it

  1. Drop the instrument onto A's tribrach, and a prism onto B's. Level carefully.
  2. Check four settings before the first shot: prism constant matches the prism; scale factor 1.000000; sea-level and projection reduction off; minimum angle reading 1″. Traps says what each one costs.
  3. Sight B and zero the horizontal circle on it.
  4. Dial the central line's angle and lock the horizontal motion. Without touching it, mark where the vertical crosshair falls on the rear bouwplank and on the front one — a pencil line, then a nail. No prism pole, no distance: the line needs only the angle.
  5. Along that same line of sight, set M0 and M1 by distance. The EDM's error runs along the line here and cannot turn it.
  6. Stake MF and the pad points GF–P4 the same way, while the tribrachs are still in place: stage 8 builds on them.
  7. The corners go by angle and distance from the table. A prism pole on a bipod is fine for them: the budget puts them within 3 mm, and the masonry holds 10 mm.
  8. Transfer to bouwplank (profile boards) set well outside the trench line, so the marks survive excavation.
  9. Sight B again at the end. If the circle no longer reads zero, something moved; re-mark from that point on.
Zeroing on B beats dialling in the azimuth for a rented instrument at 7 a.m.: a turned angle carries no absolute number to mistype. If you do orient the circle instead, set HZ to the backsight azimuth setout.py prints. On this site the central line then reads exactly 294°15′18″ (Integer-arcsecond sites). Deflection of the vertical — the difference between the instrument's gravity-levelled horizontal and the GNSS ellipsoid normal — is about 2″ here. It cancels almost entirely in a turned angle between two near-horizontal targets. Ignore it.

Before anyone digs

Verify, twice, two different ways

Tape check — shape. Measure all four sides and both diagonals.

Do this stage with the contractor, and hand over. You produced the marks, so where the house sits and which way it points are your responsibility, not the contractor's. From the handover on, the contractor's job is to keep the bouwplank intact and build to it. Take the tape and shadow checks together, write down the four sides, both diagonals, the A–B tape and the shadow result, and both sign the sheet before anyone digs. Put that split of responsibility in the contract. If a board is disturbed, the contractor stops work on that line and reports it, and you re-mark it.

Part two · Reading the sun at the house

Reading prayer times off the sun

The survey that turns the house onto the qibla also puts true north on the ground, and true north is all two small solar instruments need. They read Dhuhr and Asr from the sun at the house, and check each reading against praytime.js v3.2 to the second. Maghrib needs a measured skyline; Isha and Fajr need a photometer.

What the plans do not record. The fence and the western horizon are not in the house's drawings. The outdoor gnomon therefore stands on its own post, and uses the fence only if it is rigid. What praytime.js v3.2 actually computes

The source of praytime@3.2.0 from npm, whose tarball SHA-1 matches the registry, settles five facts that decide how to compare against it:

  • No elevation. location([lat, lng]) takes two numbers. The horizon is fixed at 0.833° (34′ refraction + 16′ semidiameter), with no dip term. The site's height above sea level does not enter the code at all.
  • Minutes by default. It rounds to the nearest minute. .round('none').format('x') returns milliseconds, and without it nothing below can be tested.
  • One iteration. The sun is evaluated at fixed guesses (Asr at 13:00 local mean time). With iterations: 5, Asr moves by −3.2…+5.8 s.
  • Dhuhr is the sun's centre on the meridian. Definition 3 adds the time the rest of the disc takes to cross, which is 63.7–70.9 s through the year, not a constant 65 s. It is longest in January, when the sun is nearest and its path is most tilted by declination; the mean is 66.8 s. adjust({dhuhr: '1.11 min'}) stays within −2.9…+4.3 s; the checker prints the exact figure for each date.
  • Asr is geometric. It has no refraction, and it uses |lat − decl| with the declination at its guess time. A real shadow is lifted by refraction, which makes it reach the Asr length +3.8…+6.4 s later. You measure the noon shadow at noon, and that differs by up to ±5.9 s from taking the declination at the Asr instant. maghrib: '2 min' is sunset + exactly 120 000 ms.

new PrayTime('Singapore') is exactly Fajr 20° / Isha 18°, the Kemenag angles.

On site · while the floor is open

Mark the gnomon lines before the walls hide them

Both instruments run on true north, not on the house axes. True north lies 24°15′18″ from +x toward +y, a 65°44′42″ clockwise turn from the central line. setout.py already printed their points in the same polar table as the corners: MF MN MS for the meridian line, and GF GP GN GS P1–P4 for the pad.

  1. Meridian line, from its own foot. Occupy the foot MF, sight B and set the HZ setout.py prints. Then 0°00′00″ is true north and 180°00′00″ true south. Mark both on boards about 3 m out: past the instrument's minimum focus and past both line ends (+0.971 / −1.902 m). With a 5″ instrument that is 0.67′ including the baseline, inside the line's 1′ allowance. Turning 65°44′42″ off the staked central line also works, at about 0.84′.
  2. Never stake MN and MS as two polar points from A with a prism pole. A 2.87 m line cannot absorb the EDM's 2 mm, and that is about 6′.
  3. Asr pad. Stake the foot pin GF, the post GP 0.30 m west of it, the noon-line ends GN/GS and the pad corners P1–P4. The pad's north–south line needs only ±0.5°. Without --pad-foot x,y the tool puts the pad 4 m past the qibla wall, a placeholder; it warns if the pad overlaps the house.
  4. Put the direction marks where they survive: nails in the ring-beam faces, or boards well beyond the room. The meridian line itself is re-set later, from the real aperture.

Dhuhr — a meridian line under the roof

Definition 3: the whole disc has cleared the meridian.

Indoor, roof aperture

At 3.20 m the sun's image crosses the line at 13.4–15.3 mm/min, so 1 mm of construction error costs 3.9–4.4 s. A small hole gives a disc with ~3 mm edges. You see it touch the line and you see it leave; leaving is definition 3. The floor need not be level, since only the line's position matters.

Outdoor, rod on fence

At 1.50 m the shadow moves 6.2–7.0 mm/min, so 1 mm costs 8.6–9.6 s. A bare rod's shadow edge is a blur as wide as the whole two-minute event, and fences sway in wind. Workable to about ±10 s with a ball nodus, but it is the weaker of the two.

Why Dhuhr is the cleanest comparison: refraction lifts the sun vertically, and on the meridian “vertical” is north–south, so the east–west crossing is untouched. Nothing atmospheric enters.

Build the meridian room

  1. Aperture. A 3 mm hole, drilled and deburred, in 0.3 mm brass or stainless shim, set in a 150 × 150 mm plate held horizontal at ceiling level, H ≈ 3.20 m. The sharpest hole for 3.2 m is 2.6 mm; 3 mm gives up a little sharpness for brightness.
  2. Support. A steel angle (for example L50×50×5) spanning between the ring beams on grid lines y = 7.0 and y = 10.3, anchored into their side faces at mid-depth, clear of the 2 + 2 D12 bars and the ⌀8 ties at 150. Never hang it from the light-steel truss or the ceiling: those move millimetres with heat and wind, and 1 mm is 4 s.
  3. Roof. Replace one sheet above the aperture with transparent polycarbonate of the same profile. Opal or translucent sheet scatters the light and destroys the image. At a height G above the aperture it must pass the noon sun from 0.30 G south to 0.59 G north of the aperture's vertical; ±0.05 G east–west covers ±10 min around noon. For G = 1 m, that is about 0.9 × 0.1 m of clear sheet.
  4. Plumb, then set the line. Drop the foot from the real hole with a laser plumb, or a heavy bob in still air, to ±0.5 mm: 1 mm east–west is 4 s. Shift the north direction from stage 8 parallel onto that point. A parallel shift needs a tape, not an instrument.
  5. Line. A 3 mm brass or stainless strip set flush in the floor, 2.873 m long: 2.822 m of image travel plus half an image at each end. For the first season, use a taut 0.5 mm steel wire so the line can still be moved.
  6. Calendar. Ticks where the image centre sits at noon: January–June on one side, July–December on the other.
  7. Check it by the sun: the Indian circle. Draw a circle about the foot, mark where the image centre crosses it before and after noon, and bisect the chord. Do it within two weeks of a solstice, when the declination barely moves. Agreement within ~2′ with the total-station line closes the loop.

Asr — a ball nodus outdoors

Shadow = height + the shadow at midday.

Why the roof tile fails. The Asr shadow is H(1 + tan z₀) long, where z₀ is the noon zenith distance. With the aperture at 3.20 m that is 3.20–5.07 m, so the image lands beyond the room's walls on every day of the year.

The one indoor variant that fits is a hole in the qibla wall at about 1.5 m. At Asr the sun is 48.6° left to 9.1° right of that wall's normal, 32–45° up, and the image lands 1.5–2.4 m into the room. But then the floor must be level to ±0.5 mm across 2 m, which a tiled floor almost never is: 1 mm per metre costs about 10 s. Outdoors you can build the surface level from scratch.

10
Any time after stage 8

Build the post and the pad

  1. Post. Rigid and free-standing: a 60 × 60 mm steel box section in a concrete footing, or a small RC pier. Mount on the fence only if it is solid masonry or concrete; panel fences sway.
  2. Nodus. A 60 mm ball on a 0.30 m horizontal arm pointing east from the post, with the ball centre exactly H = 1.500 m above the pad and at least 0.10 m above any fence top to its west. The post must end at the arm so that it never shades the ball. A ball is needed because a pinhole image outdoors is too faint to see: roughly 500 lux against the 10 000+ lux of open skylight. A ball's shadow is dark. At Asr the ball is up to 2.8 m from its shadow, where the sun's disc blurs the edge by 26 mm; at 60 mm the ball still leaves a dark core at least 34 mm wide.
  3. Pad. From the foot (the plumb point of the ball centre), east −0.04 → +2.03 m and north −1.35 → +0.84 m, i.e. 2.07 × 2.19 m. For the dry season only (15 Apr–31 Aug) a quarter-annulus of radius 1.92–2.38 m toward 107°–123° is enough. The surface should be matte and light, with every working point within ±0.5 mm of one horizontal plane (use a water-tube level and a straightedge); 1 mm of height at the arc costs 3–4.5 s.
  4. Marks. A brass pin at the foot, and a north–south line through it for the noon shadow. Here ±0.5° of azimuth is plenty, because the noon shadow's length hardly depends on it. Do not engrave fixed Asr arcs: near the equinoxes the radius changes by up to 11 mm a day, so it is set each day.

Reading

  1. Noon. Mark the centre of the ball's shadow on the north–south line, taking the midpoint of its dark core's two edges. s₀ is the distance from the foot to that mark. The checker predicts s₀; a big mismatch means H or the foot is wrong.
  2. Set today's arc. Hook a steel tape over the foot pin, set it to R = H + s₀, and scribe a short arc around today's direction (the checker prints it).
  3. Asr. Film the arc and time the dark core's front edge and back edge crossing it. Asr is the mean of the two. The shadow moves 13–19 mm/min along its length.
  4. Compare with the checker's “expect obs − code”, which already includes refraction and the noon-declination convention.

Maghrib — the limb against a measured skyline

Complete sunset + 2 minutes.

Neither the roof tile nor the rod works. A roof opening only admits sun from high in the sky. At sunset a rod's shadow just stretches and fades, telling you nothing about where the sun's edge is relative to the skyline. The event you need is the upper limb meeting the skyline, so the instrument is a camera and the calibration is the skyline itself.

11
Once · a clear evening

Measure the skyline and set up the camera station

  1. Station. Pick a spot where the skyline across 280°–294° is clear and distant; that is where the dry-season sun sets, 0.7°–14° left of the qibla. Height helps clear trees, and changes nothing in the code.
  2. Measure the skyline once, from the camera position, with the total station: its vertical angle every 0.5° of azimuth from 244° to 296°, in both faces. Any angle class is ample, but a rented instrument's vertical index error does not cancel in a single face, and it can be tens of seconds. Save the pairs as “azimuth altitude” lines; --horizon-file uses them. 1′ of skyline is 4.4 s of sunset.
  3. Record. Phone or camera on a tripod, video from three minutes before, with a time source in frame. Never look through an optical viewfinder or binoculars. The last frame with any sunlight is your sunset; Maghrib is 120 s later.
  4. Log temperature and pressure. Refraction scales with P/T, and the checker takes --T and --P.

What to expect. Against the code's sunset, a correct observation on a true 0° skyline comes 8.6–12.3 s early at 28 °C / 1005 hPa (5.5–9.2 s at 24 °C / 1010 hPa), because warm air refracts less than the code's fixed 34′. Every arcminute of skyline above 0° makes it another 4.4 s earlier. On top of that, refraction at the horizon wanders from night to night by several arcminutes, worth ±10–40 s here. So ±30 s is a fair target for the mean of many clear evenings, not for any single one.

Isha and Fajr — not a sun or shadow problem

No sun-disc or shadow method exists. With the sun 18° below the horizon, the lowest directly sunlit air above you is about 330 km up (410 km at 20°). Nothing near you is in sunlight: there is no disc to project and no shadow to cast. What the angles describe is twilight: sunlight scattered by the high atmosphere.

What you can observe is the sky's brightness against time.

  • Instrument. A sky-quality photometer logging every 30–60 s, at the zenith or about 15° above the sun's azimuth (Fajr 66°–114°, Isha 246°–294°). Or a camera in full manual (fixed ISO, shutter and aperture, RAW) on an interval timer.
  • Fajr is the first rise of the curve above its night floor. By eye, it is the horizontal white band along the eastern horizon (fajr ṣādiq), not the tilted cone of zodiacal light (fajr kādhib), which is prominent at this latitude.
  • Isha is the curve settling onto its night floor. By eye, it is the last of the western glow (shafaq).
  • Compare in degrees, not minutes. bun observe.js --from DATE --at HH:MM:SS gives the sun's depression at your instant. The code's 20° and 18° are exactly what you are testing. Here 1° is about 4.4 min. Photometric studies in Indonesia disagree by several degrees, so always quote the sky conditions with the angle.
  • Conditions. Moon below the horizon, clear sky, dry season. The light domes of Semarang (267°, 56 km) and Demak (284°, 33 km) sit exactly where the western glow fades, so Isha is the harder of the two here. The east (Blora, 90°, 54 km) is darker: start with Fajr.

What the house has to change

  • The qibla-end room becomes the meridian room: steel angle, aperture plate, one clear roof sheet, and a floor inlay. About 12 kg of steel: an L50×50×5 over the 3.07 m clear span between the ring beams is 11.6 kg, plus the plate and anchors. That is 0.03% of the roof mass, so the seismic model and its verification are unaffected. What needs the engineer's sign-off is the anchors, which go into the ring beams between their bars.
  • Keep the sunset station off the house. A 3.0 × 3.3 m, 120 mm RC roof deck is about 2.9 t at roof level against the 37.8 t modelled. It would shift T₁, the modal mass ratios and the base shear, and the structural verification would have to be re-run. A free-standing mast or platform near the west boundary leaves the verified structure alone.
  • The qibla wall faces 294°15′18″, 0.7°–14° right of every dry-season sunset (as does the gable, if the ridge runs along the long axis). A high window there gives an indoor sunset view straight along the qibla.
  • Outdoor gnomon on its own post near the west boundary, pad to its east, with the view toward 246°–303° kept clear above 30°.

        if ((fm > 0) === (fa > 0)) { a = m; fa = fm; } else b = m;
    }
    return (a + b) / 2;
}

function day(y, mo, d) {
    const t0 = Date.UTC(y, mo - 1, d) - TZ * 36e5;                  // local midnight, UT ms
    const c = code.times([y, mo, d]), cc = codeConv.times([y, mo, d]);

    const transit = bisect(t => sun(t).H, t0 + 10.5 * 36e5, t0 + 13 * 36e5);
    const n = sun(transit);
    const offMeridian = t => { const s = sun(t); return Math.cos(s.dec * rad) * Math.sin(s.H * rad); };
    const sinSD = Math.sin(n.sd * rad);
    const T1 = bisect(t => offMeridian(t) + sinSD, transit - 3e5, transit);   // leading limb reaches the plane
    const T2 = bisect(t => offMeridian(t) - sinSD, transit, transit + 3e5);   // trailing limb leaves it (Dhuhr)

    const altNoonApp = n.alt + refr(n.alt);
    const z0 = 90 - altNoonApp;
    const asrAlt = Math.atan(1 / (1 + Math.tan(z0 * rad))) * deg;           // apparent
    const asr = bisect(t => { const s = sun(t); return s.alt + refr(s.alt) - asrAlt; }, transit + 6e4, transit + 6 * 36e5);

    const set = bisect(t => {
        const s = sun(t), limb = s.alt + s.sd;
        return limb + refr(limb) - skyline(s.az);
    }, transit + 3 * 36e5, transit + 8 * 36e5);

    const gnomon = H => {
        const s0 = H * Math.tan(z0 * rad);
        const sa = sun(asr), aApp = (sa.alt + refr(sa.alt)) * rad;
        const vNoon = H * Math.cos(n.dec * rad) * (2 * Math.PI / 86400) / Math.sin(altNoonApp * rad) * 60e3; // mm/min
        const a2 = sun(asr + 6e4), a2App = (a2.alt + refr(a2.alt)) * rad;
        const vAsr = (H / Math.tan(a2App) - H / Math.tan(aApp)) * 1e3;                             // mm/min
        return {
            noonOffset: s0, noonSide: n.dec > LAT ? 'S' : 'N',
            noonImage: [H / Math.sin(altNoonApp * rad) * 2 * n.sd * rad * 1e3, H / Math.sin(altNoonApp * rad) ** 2 * 2 * n.sd * rad * 1e3],
            vNoon, asrRadius: H + s0, asrDir: mod(sa.az + 180, 360), vAsr,
            asrImageRadial: H / Math.sin(aApp) ** 2 * 2 * n.sd * rad * 1e3,
        };
    };
    return { c, cc, transit, T1, T2, semidur: (T2 - transit) / 1e3, asr, set, maghrib: set + 12e4, z0, asrAlt,
             setAz: sun(set).az, gin: gnomon(H_IN), gout: gnomon(H_OUT) };
}

Configuring and logging the board

What stage 2 needs from each PX1125R, and how to know the hour was not wasted.

  1. Configure once, at the desk. Connect the EVB's USB, which enumerates as a serial port. In SkyTraq's GNSS Viewer, put the RTK mode to base. Its survey-in position does not matter, since nothing downstream uses it. Enable RTCM 3 output of 1077, 1097, 1117 and 1127 (MSM7 for GPS, Galileo, QZSS and BeiDou) plus 1005, at 1 Hz. Save to SRAM and flash, so the setting survives the power bank being unplugged.
  2. Record with RTKLIB's str2str on whatever the board is plugged into: 
    str2str -in serial://ttyUSB0:115200 -out file://A.rtcm3     # Linux; ttyACM0 on some hosts
str2str -in serial://COM5:115200    -out file://A.rtcm3     # Windows
    115200 baud is the module's default. Four constellations of MSM7 at 1 Hz use a fraction of it.
  3. Before you walk away, confirm the file is growing, and that GNSS Viewer shows L5, E5a and B2a tracked, not just L1. Note the start time in local time and UTC. The date is what convbin's -tr needs later.
  4. Stop cleanly. Stop str2str before unplugging, so the last buffer is written.
The raw-binary alternative. The board can also stream SkyTraq raw binary, which SkyTraq's Raw Measurement to RINEX Converter turns into RINEX. Use it if the RTCM path ever gives you trouble. Do not feed that binary to RTKLIB's own -r stq decoder, which was written for an older SkyTraq raw format.

Converting to RINEX

convbin -r rtcm3 -tr 2026/10/04 08:00:00 -v 3.04 -od -os -o A.obs A.rtcm3
  • -tr is the approximate start, in GPS time, near enough to pick the right week. MSM carries only time-of-week; without it, a file converted a week later lands a week wrong.
  • -od -os keep Doppler and signal strength. RTKLIB uses the Doppler in slip detection, and the SNR mask needs the signal strength.
  • No -n. The base-mode output list carries no ephemeris messages, so the navigation data comes from the Ina-CORS .nav file. Both runs use it.
  • Open A.obs and check two things: the SYS / # / OBS TYPES lines list L5/E5a/B2a observables, and the epochs sit on whole seconds.

RTKCONV, the GUI, does the same job, and its RTCM options have the same approximate-time field.

Which datum and epoch you are working in

SRGI2013 is a semi-dynamic datum. Coordinates refer to a fixed reference epoch, and a deformation model carries them forward or back. Indonesia sits at the junction of several plates, so this is tens of centimetres over a decade.

  • SRGI2013 epoch 2012.0 is the original realisation, tied to ITRF2008; epoch 2021.0 is tied to ITRF2014.
  • BIG's deformation model is built from Ina-CORS and JKG displacement vectors, kriged on a 0.1° grid and bilinearly interpolated to your point, through the Transformasi Epok menu.
  • Going from epoch 2012.0 to 2021.0 is two steps: ITRF2008 → ITRF2014 at the same epoch first, then the epoch transformation. Not one.
None of this touches the azimuth. A and B drift together, so the vector between them is unchanged. The qibla bearing moves 2.1×10−6 degrees per metre of absolute error. The epoch matters for the coordinate you file with the certificate, not for the setout. Get it right for the record, and do not let it hold up the setout.

For the record: RTKLIB reports latitude, longitude and ellipsoidal height in the frame and epoch of the base coordinate you entered. Apply the antenna height if you did not enter it as ant1-antdelu. Transform to your target epoch with SRGI's Transformasi & Konversi Koordinat, which also gives UTM 49S or TM-3. For height above sea level, use BIG's INAGEOID2020, not RTKLIB's built-in EGM96, which is nowhere near good enough for Indonesia.

Reading and trusting the result

In the .pos output, Q=1 is fixed: integer ambiguities resolved, the millimetre-class result. Q=2 is float, decimetres at best, and Q=5 is single-point. Check the AR ratio is comfortably above 3, and more importantly that the fix held through the back half of the session instead of flickering. The sdE/sdN/sdU columns are formal uncertainties, not guarantees.

  1. Split the hour. Process the first and second halves of the session separately. Agreement of B-against-A within a couple of millimetres beats any single run's statistics, because it tests the real multipath instead of the filter's opinion of itself.
  2. Tape A–B. A steel tape checks the baseline length to a few millimetres, with no GNSS involved. It cannot check the direction; it does catch a gross error.
  3. BIG's online post-processing is a free cross-check for A's absolute position. It may choose a different base than you did; if it rejects the file, that is not evidence the data is bad.
  4. The shadow check in stage 7 is the independent test of direction.

Choosing the total station

What the rented instrument has to be good enough for on this job, and where paying for a better one stops showing up in the house.

The instrument's whole task is to carry the A→B azimuth from B onto the ground: orient on B, dial the line, and let someone mark it. The LocalCartesian ENU frame about A in which setout.py computes every point is flat to within 0.1″ across the parcel: meridian convergence over 25 m east of A is 0.099″. A plane-surveying instrument therefore carries it with no projection correction at all — provided it applies none of its own (Traps). The model is in dsygv/setout/tsspec.py, and setout.py --selftest checks it against a simulation of the staking itself.

How to read a spec sheet

  • Angle accuracy is quoted to ISO 17123-3 (or DIN 18723): the standard deviation of one horizontal direction measured in both faces. Staking is single-face, so one pointing is √2 times the sheet figure, and an angle between two pointings twice it. A 5″ instrument turns a staked angle to about 10″. On Topcon and Sokkia models the last digit is usually the class — GM-55, CX-105, iM-55 are 5″; GM-52, CX-102, iM-52 are 2″ — but confirm it on the sheet.
  • Distance accuracy is ±(a mm + b ppm × D), typically 2 mm + 2 ppm to a prism and 3 mm + 2 ppm reflectorless. Inside a parcel the ppm part is nothing: 2 ppm over 50 m is 0.1 mm. Compare instruments on the fixed part.

Ways to be wrong by more than everything above

Each of these dwarfs the entire error budget. Check them before you worry about anything else.

Centreline vs. wall face 100–115 mm

Every coordinate the tool emits is a column or wall centreline, matching the grid that Geometry.kt analyses. The 200 mm shear walls put outer faces at x = −0.100 and 6.100; the 230 mm columns put faces at y = −0.115 and 10.415. Agree with the mandor which one the bouwplank carries, out loud, before anyone strings a line.

Prism constant 17.5–30 mm

A prism is 0, −17.5 or −30 mm, and the instrument has to be told which. A mismatch moves every staked distance by the difference. It cannot turn the line when A is on it, but it moves M0, M1 and every corner.

Instrument scale factor 400 ppm = 5.3 mm

ENU distances are ground distances. An instrument left on a UTM scale factor (0.9996) or a sea-level reduction shrinks every one of them. Over 13.3 m that is 5.3 mm. Set the scale to 1.000000 and the reductions off.

BPN TM-3 grid north −10.39′ / +11.45′

That is the frame your parcel certificate is in, and its grid north is not true north. At this site it is off by more than ten arcminutes, in opposite directions depending on whether you are in the CM 109.5° or CM 112.5° zone. Work in ENU, where north is geodetic north, and never take a bearing off the certificate.

Prism pole on a short line 85″ to 6′

A hand-held pole's bubble allows about 3 mm. On the 10.3 m central line that is 85″. On the 2.87 m meridian line, with the EDM's error added, it is 6′. Lines are marked by crosshair. Poles are for points.

A spherical qibla formula +7.40′

Great-circle-on-a-sphere is wrong by more than seven arcminutes here — larger than your whole measurement budget. Use the ellipsoidal geodesic. A rhumb line, if anyone suggests one, is out by 135′.

pos1-navsys = 13 no BeiDou

13 is GPS + GLONASS + Galileo. It turns on the one system the board does not track and turns off BeiDou, which it does. The value is a bit mask: GPS 1, SBAS 2, GLONASS 4, Galileo 8, QZSS 16, BeiDou 32. This board wants 57.

RTCM without a date no fix, ever

Convert an MSM log without -tr and convbin may put it in the wrong GPS week. Base and rover then never share an epoch, and the solution fails with no helpful message.

Unmatched epoch intervals float solution

The CORS logs at 30 s and your board at 1 Hz. Left alone, most rover epochs get differenced against stale base data, and the solution may never fix. Decimate with -ti 30, or let pos2-maxage pair within tolerance — but choose one deliberately.

Questions and answers

Can a PX1125R board really deliver a millimetre-class baseline?

For a short static baseline with a fixed solution, yes. SkyTraq specifies 1 cm + 1 ppm for RTK, and a static hour over 25 m averages well below that. What decides it is the antenna and the site, not the module: an active L1/L5 antenna, a ground plane if it is a bare patch, open sky, and the same antenna model turned the same way on both marks.

Does the PX1125R track GLONASS?

The standard part does not. It tracks GPS/QZSS L1/L5, Galileo E1/E5a, BeiDou B1I/B2a and NavIC L5; the PX1125R-01 variant adds GLONASS L1. Leave GLONASS out either way. Its inter-channel bias between different receivers is the most common reason a CORS-based solution will not fix, and navsys = 57 excludes it.

Why log RTCM3 MSM7 instead of SkyTraq raw binary?

RTKLIB decodes RTCM3 MSM natively, and MSM7 carries full-precision phase. RTKLIB's SkyTraq decoder was written for an older raw format than this module's, so the binary path may produce nothing. If you do log binary, convert it with SkyTraq's own Raw Measurement to RINEX Converter.

With two boards, do I need the CORS at all?

For the azimuth, no. The A→B baseline is processed directly, and the qibla bearing moves only 2.1×10−6 degrees per metre of error in A's absolute position. You still want A's coordinate to about a metre for the qibla computation, and properly for the record. That is what the CORS run gives you.

How long do I need to log?

An hour with two boards on a 25 m baseline is generous. Ambiguities on a line that short fix in seconds; the hour is for averaging multipath, which decorrelates only over hundreds of seconds. With one board against a CORS 14.5 km away, take one to two hours per mark. Split either into halves at the desk for a repeatability check.

Which total station should I rent?

A 5″ total station with a detachable tribrach and a prism with its own tribrach adapter for the backsight. It is enough on every parcel from 8 × 15 to 20 × 40 m. Book it for the day you observe, so it can drop onto the GNSS tribrach without re-centring. For the qibla line alone, a 5–10″ digital theodolite and a steel tape are also enough.

Is a 1″ instrument worth it?

No. On a 25 m baseline it improves the central line from 32.3″ to 30.8″, 0.08 mm over 10.3 m. Forced centring and marking the line on the boards by crosshair each matter more than the whole difference between classes.

Does an integer-arcsecond parcel make the setout more accurate?

By at most half an arcsecond, which is one sixtieth of the GNSS error. The keypad takes whole arcseconds, and the integer site means the qibla and the 65°44′42″ turn to true north need no rounding. The backsight still does. Its real value is that the display shows exactly the published number, so anyone can audit it.

Why does the epoch of my coordinate matter in Indonesia?

SRGI2013 is semi-dynamic and the ground moves: tens of centimetres a decade. Epoch 2012.0 ties to ITRF2008 and 2021.0 to ITRF2014, and converting between them is two steps. It matters for the coordinate you file with the certificate, and not at all for the azimuth, because A and B move together.

Can I use PPP instead of a base station?

For A's absolute position, as a cross-check, yes. For the azimuth it is the wrong tool: two simultaneous boards 25 m apart cancel the atmosphere and the orbits outright, where PPP has to model them and converge.

Why a meridian line indoors for Dhuhr but a ball outdoors for Asr?

A 3 mm hole 3.20 m above the floor throws a sharp sun image that crosses a line at 13 to 15 mm a minute, which is what makes Dhuhr good to about 4 seconds. The Asr shadow from that height would be 3.20 to 5.07 m long, beyond the room's walls every day of the year. Outdoors a pinhole image is too faint to see against open skylight, so the Asr gnomon uses a 60 mm ball whose shadow has a dark core.

Why does an observed Dhuhr come about a minute after the code's?

praytime.js v3.2 gives the moment the sun's centre crosses the meridian. Definition 3 waits for the whole disc to clear it, which takes another 63.7 to 70.9 seconds depending on the date, not a constant 65. The checker prints the exact figure for each day, so the comparison is against that, not against zero.

Can a shadow measure Isha or Fajr?

No. With the sun 18 to 20 degrees below the horizon, the lowest sunlit air is 330 to 410 km up, so nothing near you casts a shadow. What can be measured is the sky's brightness against time, with a sky-quality photometer or a camera on fixed settings, compared with the code in degrees of solar depression rather than in minutes.
