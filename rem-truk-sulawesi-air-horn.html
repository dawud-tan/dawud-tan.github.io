Rem truk Sulawesi on real air: a six-trumpet air horn whose notes slide in

The "rem truk Sulawesi" clip is a horn melody in which every note starts low and slides up into pitch. This page designs a machine that plays it with compressed air, not a speaker: one tuned horn per pitch, a telescoping trumpet on each that shortens as the note starts, 12 solenoid valves and a small controller. Every dimension and timing comes from the physical simulation that renders the same tune as a WAV.

Status 7 November 2026: designed from a physical simulation; not yet built Tune 33 notes, G5 to D#6, 90 BPM, 13.3 s Model lip-reed horn with waveguide trumpet, 96 kHz Reading time ~14 min

The short answer

  • Six diaphragm horns, one per pitch: G5, G#5, A#5, C6, D6 and D#6. The tune is a single line, so only one horn sounds at a time.
  • The scoop is mechanical: each trumpet is a telescoping tube. A pneumatic cylinder pulls it from long to short between two adjustable hard stops. The short stop sets the note; the long stop sets how low the note starts.
  • The slide alone is not enough with a stiff diaphragm. In the simulation a longer tube bends it only about 2 semitones before the horn chokes. Either link the diaphragm's tension to the slide (a −8.6 semitone scoop from a 1.9× tube), or fit a softer diaphragm (about 6 semitones, and simpler to build).
  • Air: the horns run at 0.10 bar through a precision regulator and the slides at 4–6 bar. On a truck, take the air from the brake system only through a pressure-protection valve.
  • Timing: a microcontroller switches the 12 valves on a 166.7 ms grid. The horn valve stays open N × 166.7 − 30 ms; the slide valve opens 20 ms after it.
  • Expect: about 10 L of free air per play, and tuning to a few cents. Pitch moves about 3 cents per °C and about 7 cents per kPa of horn pressure.
  • On Indonesian roads: a horn must stay within 83–118 dB (PP 55/2012), and since March 2024 buses with telolet horns fail inspection.

1What the horn plays

The clip shared as "rem truk Sulawesi" sounds at C6 to G#6. Its phrase comes from the song "Mezmar El Asfour" by Mostafa Bazet, which plays it five semitones lower, from G5 to D#6. This horn plays in the song's key. The melody is someone else's composition; below is only the 13-second excerpt the machine plays.

The excerpt is 33 notes at 90 BPM, so a sixteenth note lasts 166.7 ms. Lengths are counted in sixteenths, and - is a rest:

Only six pitches occur, so the machine needs six horns. C6 is the busiest, with 9 of the 33 notes.

2The machine at a glance

3One horn, up close

An air-horn head works like a brass player's lips. Air at 0.10 bar fills the chamber and pushes the diaphragm off the end of the nozzle. Air then rushes through the gap, about 0.15 mm at rest, and into the trumpet. The pressure wave runs to the bell, comes back and pushes the diaphragm shut, and the cycle repeats at the trumpet's resonance. A longer air column gives a lower note.

The trumpet

A fixed inner tube with an 8 mm bore leaves the nozzle. An outer tube with a small bell slides over it on a carriage. The carriage runs on a rail, so the tubes carry no load and only need a close sliding fit, like a trombone slide. Make both tubes about as long as the tuned air column: fully nested at the short stop, overlapping about 10 mm at the long stop. That way one stage stretches to about 1.9× its length.

The stops

Two adjustable hard stops on the rail define the two lengths the tube ever needs. The short stop is the tuning screw: the note is held while the carriage sits against it, so its pitch does not depend on how fast the slide moved. The long stop sets how low the note starts. Urethane bumpers keep the stops from clicking.

  • The lengths are acoustic: the model's cylinder plus its bell reflection. A real bell and the bore step between the tubes will shift them, so give each stop about ±15 mm of adjustment and tune by ear (section 8).
  • A quarter wavelength is shorter than the tuned tube: 10.9 cm for G5 against 11.4 cm. The diaphragm pulls the pitch up, so the tube must be longer to compensate.
  • The start lengths are 1.94× the tuned ones, slightly more than one telescope stage reaches. At 1.9× the note starts 8.6 semitones low instead of 8.8; at 1.8×, 7.9 semitones.

5The scoop: what it takes

What the WAV does

In the simulated WAV, the horn valve opens and the chamber fills, with a 10 ms time constant. The piston waits 20 ms, until the horn sounds fully, then shortens the tube to its tuned length over 60 ms, fastest at first (a cubic ease-out). The note lands at about 80 ms. What a machine needs

The WAV's model changes three things at once during the scoop: tube length, diaphragm tension and chamber pressure. A machine should change as few as possible, so each was tested in the simulator on its own (G5 shown; D#6 behaves the same within 0.1 semitone):

What changesResult in the simulator

Option A: link the diaphragm

This is the WAV, mechanically. Make each diaphragm a thin shim of the table's mass per area, stretched like a drumhead on a tension ring. A lever on the slide carriage tightens it as the tube shortens, like a timpani pedal: from 36 % to 100 % of its final tension, so its resonance rises from 0.6 to 1.0 of its final value. For an even level, the chamber should start at 0.6× its pressure too. Without that, the model's scoop starts 6–7 dB louder than the held note. Result: the full scoop, about 8.6 semitones from a 1.9× tube.

Option B: a softer diaphragm

Build this one first. Use a fixed diaphragm whose resonance is about two-thirds of its note, so the trumpet, not the diaphragm, decides the pitch. The slide alone then carries about 6 semitones of scoop, and nothing else moves. The top of the horn's range is where it nearly chokes, so the short stop needs care. The simulator has not calibrated option B's lengths: set both stops by ear.

Speed, shape and repeated notes

  • Speed: a 60 ms glide over 6.7–10.6 cm averages 1.1–1.8 m/s. Keep the moving part light, under about 50 g with thin-wall brass or aluminium. A 10 mm bore at 5 bar gives about 39 N, far more than it needs.
  • Shape: a pneumatic slide moves in an S-curve: it speeds up, then cushions. The WAV's glide is fastest at the start. Shape the motion with the flow controls and bumpers, and judge by ear.
  • Repeated notes: four places repeat a pitch back to back: G5, C6, A#5 and G#5. There the slide has only the 30 ms gap to travel back 8–11 cm, about 3 m/s, which is at the top of what small pneumatic cylinders do. Drive the return hard, with quick-exhaust valves. If those notes still scoop short, add a second horn for each of the four pitches (10 horns in all) and alternate between them.

6Air supply

  • On a truck: take air from the brake circuit (8–10 bar) only through a pressure-protection valve, which shuts below about 6.5 bar. Then the horns can never take the air the brakes need.
  • Standalone: a 12 V compressor, a 10 L tank, a pressure switch and a safety valve. Between 8 and 6 bar the tank holds about 20 L of usable air: two plays before the compressor must catch up.
  • Use per play: each horn passes about 0.5 L/s at 0.10 bar and the valves are open 11.2 s of the 13.3 s, about 6 L. The slides, with 10 mm bores at 5 bar, take about 3 L more. With the hoses, budget about 10 L of free air.
  • Horn pressure: 0.10 bar is about what a trumpet player blows. More pressure is louder and sharper: in the model, 20 kPa is 8 dB louder and 51 cents sharp, and at 30 kPa the second harmonic becomes the strongest partial, so the horn sounds brighter. Pick the pressure first, then tune at it.
  • Hoses: mount the horn valves on the heads. The hose between valve and head is volume that has to fill and empty with every note.

7Controller and timing

Any microcontroller with 12 outputs can play the tune: an Arduino Nano or a Raspberry Pi Pico switching logic-level MOSFETs, with a flyback diode across every coil. The timing is a fixed grid:

sixteenth = 166.7 ms                       (90 BPM)
for each note (pitch, n) in the sequence:
    t = 0                horn valve[pitch] on
    t = 20 ms - lag      slide valve[pitch] on   (lag: that slide's measured delay)
    t = n x 166.7 - 30   both off                (a sixteenth sounds 136.7 ms)
    next note at t = n x 166.7
for each rest (n):       wait n x 166.7 ms
after the last note:     2 sixteenths of silence (333 ms)

The lag is how long a slide valve and its cylinder take to start moving, typically 5–15 ms. Measure it once per horn (section 8) and store it per horn, so every slide starts 20 ms after its note does.

8Tuning and commissioning

  1. Set the horn pressure. Set the precision regulator to 0.10 bar, or your chosen pressure, with a gauge at a head while it sounds.
  2. Tune each note. Pin the carriage at its short stop, sound the horn and move the stop until a tuner reads the note within ±3 cents. Tune at the temperature where the horn will be used: about 3 cents per °C.
  3. Set the scoop depth. Pin the carriage at its long stop and move the stop until the horn starts as low as you want: 0.6 × the note for the full scoop (G5: 470 Hz), or as low as it still speaks.
  4. Option A only: set the tension link. At the long stop, adjust the lever until the horn speaks cleanly at the start pitch. At the short stop, recheck the tuning.
  5. Measure each slide's lag. Record a few notes with a phone at 48 kHz. The lag is the time from the horn's onset to the slide's first pitch movement, minus the firmware's wait. Set the wait to 20 ms minus the lag.
  6. Set the speeds. Adjust the flow controls until the glide takes about 60 ms and the return finishes inside the 30 ms gap. Check the four repeated notes.
  7. Play the whole tune. Record it and compare it with the WAV, note by note.

9Road rules and safety

  • Loudness: PP 55/2012, Article 69, requires a vehicle horn to be at least 83 dB and at most 118 dB.
  • Telolet horns: in March 2024, after a child was killed by a bus at the Merak port access road, the land transport directorate asked bus operators to stop using telolet horns. Following the National Transportation Safety Committee (KNKT), it warned that they can exhaust the air supply and weaken the brakes. Buses fitted with them no longer pass periodic inspection.
  • Brakes first: the pressure-protection valve is not optional on a vehicle; it is the exact failure the ministry warned about.
  • Compressed air: use rated hoses and fittings, a safety valve on every tank, and release the pressure before working on the machine.
  • Hearing: 118 dB at close range harms hearing within seconds. Test with ear protection, away from people.

10Where the numbers come from

The design follows a physical simulation of exactly this machine. The same model renders the tune as a ringtone WAV, so the WAV is what the machine should sound like. Per note it simulates:

  • The valve and chamber: the chamber fills exponentially (10 ms) when the valve opens and empties (6 ms) when it closes.
  • The diaphragm: a damped mass-spring driven by the difference between chamber and nozzle pressure. The flow through its gap follows Bernoulli and is solved exactly at every sample.
  • The trumpet: a digital waveguide with a cubic Lagrange fractional delay. The bell reflects low frequencies (an inverting lowpass at 1.8 × the note) and lets high ones out. The radiated sound is the time derivative of the flow at the bell.
  • The slide: the delay shortens during the scoop. In the WAV, diaphragm tension and chamber pressure follow it (option A).
  • Calibration: a secant search sets each tube's length until the held pitch matches equal temperament, and each start length until the first audible pitch is 0.6 × the note. The model runs at 96 kHz.

The values are physically plausible, not measured from a particular horn: 10 kPa, an 8 mm nozzle, and diaphragms equivalent to 0.2–0.5 mm steel. The model says which mechanisms matter and roughly how big things are. The exact lengths come from the tuner, which is why every stop is adjustable.

11FAQ

What is the "rem truk Sulawesi" sound?

A short horn melody shared on TikTok and YouTube as "rem truk Sulawesi": 33 notes at about 90 BPM between C6 and G#6, each note sliding up into pitch. The phrase comes from the song "Mezmar El Asfour" by Mostafa Bazet, which plays it five semitones lower, from G5 to D#6.

How does a musical air horn play a melody?

With one trumpet per pitch, each behind its own solenoid valve. A controller opens the valves in sequence. This tune uses six different pitches, so it needs six horns, and only one sounds at a time.

Can an air horn slide into a note like a slide whistle?

Yes, if its trumpet changes length while it sounds. In the simulation, a telescoping tube 1.9 times its tuned length starts the note 8.6 semitones low, provided the diaphragm's tension follows the slide. With a softer, fixed diaphragm the slide alone gives about 6 semitones.

Why can't a slowly opening valve make the scoop?

Because pressure barely moves a diaphragm horn's pitch. In the model, 2 kPa sounds only 88 cents below 10 kPa, less than a semitone, while the scoop spans almost nine semitones.

How long should an air-horn trumpet be for G5 (784 Hz)?

About 11.4 cm of air column in the model, a little longer than the quarter wavelength of 10.9 cm because the diaphragm pulls the pitch up. A real bell shifts this, so make the length adjustable and tune it with a tuner at the working pressure.

What air pressure does a musical air horn need?

This design runs the horns at 0.10 bar, about what a trumpet player blows, and the slide cylinders at 4 to 6 bar. Higher horn pressure is louder and sharper: 20 kPa is 8 dB louder and 51 cents sharp in the model, so choose the pressure first and tune at it.

Can I take the air from a truck's brake system?

Only through a pressure-protection valve that shuts below about 6.5 bar, so the horns can never use air the brakes need. Indonesia's transport ministry cited exactly this risk in 2024: telolet horns can exhaust the air supply and weaken braking.

Why use 3/2 valves for the horns instead of 2/2 valves?

A 3/2 valve vents the horn head when it closes, so the note stops in about 6 ms. A 2/2 valve leaves the air in the hose to bleed out through the horn, and the note droops at the end.

How much air does one play of the tune use?

About 10 litres of free air: roughly 6 for the horns at 0.10 bar, 3 for the slide cylinders at 5 bar and the rest in hoses. A 10 litre tank between 8 and 6 bar covers about two plays.

How accurately can a mechanical air horn be tuned?

To a few cents. Pitch moves about 3 cents per degree Celsius as the speed of sound changes, and about 7 cents per kPa of horn pressure. All horns drift together with temperature, so the tune stays in tune with itself.

Is a musical air horn legal on Indonesian roads?

PP 55/2012 requires a vehicle horn to be between 83 and 118 dB. Since March 2024 the land transport directorate has told bus operators not to use telolet horns, and buses fitted with them fail periodic inspection.

Why is the design based on a simulation?

The tune was first rendered as a ringtone WAV by simulating this machine: valve, diaphragm, trumpet, bell and slide. The dimensions and timings come from the same model. Its parameters are plausible rather than measured from a particular horn, so every length is adjustable and tuned by ear.
