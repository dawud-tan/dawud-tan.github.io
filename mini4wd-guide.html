Prerequisites, stated honestly. Most of this document needs less background than it looks. §4 and §5 — the rollover threshold and the traction limit, which between them decide almost everything — use only senior-high-school physics: circular motion (a = v²/r), friction (F = μN), and moments about a pivot. If you can solve a banked-curve problem, you can read them in full. University-level work is needed only for §2 and §7.5 (back-EMF, from an introductory circuits course) and §6 and §11 (rigid-body dynamics and numerical integration). The learning ladder in the practical guide maps each section to the specific topic that unlocks it.
Where the university half leads: battery-electric vehicles. Back-EMF, rigid-body dynamics and numerical integration are not Mini 4WD trivia. Nearly unchanged, they are how a battery-electric vehicle (BEV) works, and §11.9's Stage 2 is already one in miniature: a lithium pack, an ESC that is a small three-phase inverter, a brushless permanent-magnet motor, and a controller running slip control. Whatever you learn for this page, you will use again there.
  • Back-EMF (§2, §7.5). Most BEV traction motors are permanent-magnet machines, and their back-EMF rises with speed exactly as E = Ke·ω does here. The inverter can hold full torque only until that back-EMF, plus the voltage it takes to push full current through the windings (at a traction motor's frequencies mostly their inductance, ω·L·I, which grows with speed too — not §2's I·R), has used up the pack voltage. That is the base speed — the same voltage limit that sets v_nl, but met at full current rather than at §2.3's zero current, and so at a lower speed. Above it the inverter has to weaken the motor's field with a current that opposes the magnets', trading torque for speed. §7.5's governor is regeneration: above v_nl the current runs back into the pack and the motor brakes the car, which is what a BEV's inverter does on purpose, at almost any speed. And §7.5's battery sag does the same to a BEV: base speed scales with pack voltage, so peak power falls as the pack runs down.
  • Rigid-body dynamics (§4, §6). §4.3 is already the road-vehicle rollover threshold, and a battery laid flat under the floor is the lever a BEV pulls on it: less height between the centre of gravity and the pivot, which is what §4 buys with roller height. §6's bookkeeping of moments of inertia and angular momentum, turned to the vertical axis and given the one thing §6.1's airborne car never has — a moment from outside it — is what a BEV with one motor per rear wheel uses to steer with torque: driving the outside wheel harder makes the road push that side forward harder, and the difference times half the track is a yaw moment on the body (torque vectoring). And §5's μ·g ceiling binds a powerful all-wheel-drive BEV at launch just as it binds this four-wheel-drive car out of a corner (with two driven wheels, the ceiling is only their share of it), so its traction control is a limit on motor current that the inverter applies within milliseconds — Stage 2's slip control, full size.
  • Numerical integration (§11, the calculator's tab 7). The calculator's RK4 loop, integrating m·dv/dt in 0.1 ms steps, is the same kind of computation that turns a drive cycle into energy used and a range estimate before the car exists, from the same aero and rolling terms as §3 — and §8's coast-down fit is how a real car's road load is measured before its range is tested on a dynamometer. A battery-management system estimates state of charge by integrating current (coulomb counting) and checks that integral against something independent — each cell's voltage, allowing for the sag under load that §7.5 describes — much as §11.7 checked the calculator's RK4 against §11.6's closed form. The difference is that the calculator needed checking once and a BMS never stops: what goes wrong is not its integrator but the current it is fed, and a small sensor offset integrated for hours drifts.

Five scenarios, two design chains

Which chain applies depends on one question: does the layout have corners that can throw the car off? If yes, rollover geometry caps your speed and the motor is sized down to sit under it. If no, the strip length caps it instead, and the answer inverts.

Scenario D is the surprise. Under about 10 m, a 3-turn grenade motor and a 7-turn touring motor record identical times to three decimal places, because neither ever reaches its ceiling — the run is pure traction-limited acceleration at μg. Winding a special motor for a short strip buys nothing. §11.5 derives the crossover.

Scenario C's surprise is at the far end. A tuned car crosses a 30 m line at 21 m/s carrying the energy of a 22 m fall, and braking through its own tires would need about as long as the strip itself. The finish needs a designed catch, not a towel against a wall — §11.8. §11.6 is what still limits the trap speed once the motor is out of the way, and §11.7 is when and where that speed arrives.

Scenario E has no rulebook at all — only physics. It builds §11.6's hardware — sealed body, wing, suction fan — into one car, and it is the one scenario where ballast is simply wrong: a fan adds normal force without adding mass, so every gram of ballast costs time. Its second stage drops the brushed motor and the skirt gap as well, and turns into a car limited by power rather than grip. The moment an organizer publishes restrictions, E stops applying. §11.9 is the build.

The finding in four lines

  • Mass cancels from both binding constraints. Ballast does not stabilise cornering and does not improve acceleration.
  • Neither binding constraint contains a motor term. Top speed is capped by rollover geometry; corner exit is capped by tire grip.
  • Roller contact height relative to CoG height is the highest-value adjustment available, and it costs nothing but shims.
  • The self-destructing "grenade" motor solves a problem Mini 4WD does not have — except on a straight-line drag layout, where it remains correct.

                2. Motor and tractive force

                2.1 Current from back-EMF

                E   = Ke · ω
I   = (V − Ke·ω) / R_total
ω   = G · v / R_w

                2.2 Force at the tire

                τ_motor = Kt · I
τ_wheel = η_d · G · τ_motor
F_tract = τ_wheel / R_w

F_tract = (η_d · G · Kt / R_w) · (V − Ke·G·v/R_w) / R_total

                This is linear in v. Write it as F_tract = F₀ − c·v:

                F₀ = η_d · G · Kt · V   / (R_w  · R_total)
c  = η_d · G² · Kt · Ke / (R_w² · R_total)

                c is the slope — how much force the motor loses per m/s of speed. It reappears in §7.5 as the governor stiffness, where it turns out to be the most useful number in this document.

                2.3 No-load speed

                v_nl = V · R_w / (Ke · G)        (equivalently F₀ / c)

                Note what v_nl does not contain: R_total, η_d, or m. No-load speed is pure Kv-and-gearing. This is the equation that governs the whole design.

                3. Longitudinal resistance

                Three terms oppose motion. Two scale as v².

                F_aero = ½ · ρ · Cd · A · v²        = 0.0014 · v²
F_roll = Crr · m · g                = 0.038          (constant)
F_wall = μ_r · N_w = μ_r · m · v²/r = 0.0098 · v²    (corners only)

                F_wall is roller drag against the barrier. The wall normal force is itself m·v²/r, so this term has the same v² scaling as aero but a coefficient about 7× larger. On a curved layout it is the dominant resistance, and it is invisible if you only budget for aerodynamic drag.

                3.1 Terminal velocity

                C·v² + c·v + (F_roll − F₀) = 0

v_term = [ −c + √(c² + 4C(F₀ − F_roll)) ] / (2C)

                Worked for a high-Kv reference motor (Kv 6,650, giving F₀ = 20.9 N, c = 0.95 — inputs listed in §10.1):

                ConditionCv_termΣF_resistc·v
                Straight0.001421.3 m/s0.67 N20.3 N
                In corner0.011118.1 m/s3.71 N17.2 N

                The back-EMF term dominates by 30× on a straight and 4.6× in a corner. The car is Kv-limited, not drag-limited.

                Read the corner column carefully. It is tempting to treat all the resistance terms as negligible, but F_wall alone reaches 3.2 N at the corner terminal velocity — it is only under 0.5 N below about 7 m/s. The car is still Kv-limited, but by a margin of 4.6×, not the 30× the straight-line column suggests.

                3.2 Peak power — two different numbers

                P_shaft,max = V² / (4 · R_total)  ≈ 144 W   (Kv 6,650 reference, at the shaft)
P_wheel,max = η_d · P_shaft,max   ≈ 115 W   (what reaches the track)

                P_shaft correctly carries no η_d, because gearbox loss is downstream of the shaft. Don't mix them. And note P_max is independent of turn count — N sets only the RPM at which peak power occurs, not its magnitude.

                4. Cornering and wall climb — the binding constraint

                The car in a corner is pressed against the outer barrier. The roller contacts the wall at height h_r. The question is when the car levers itself up the wall.

                4.1 Force balance

                Horizontal:  N_w = m · v² / r
Vertical:    ΣN_tire = m · g

                4.2 Moment balance

                Take moments about the roller-to-wall contact point. In the corner's rotating frame the centripetal requirement becomes an outward pseudo-force acting through the CoG at height h_g, giving it a lever arm of (h_g − h_r) above the pivot. Weight acts at horizontal distance b.

                Overturning:  (m · v²/r) · (h_g − h_r)
Restoring:    m · g · b

(m·v²/r)(h_g − h_r) > m·g·b

                4.3 The result

                Both moments are linear in m, so mass cancels — this is forced, not a modelling choice:

                v_crit = √( g · r · b / (h_g − h_r) )

                Structurally this is the standard vehicle rollover threshold (v ≈ √(g·r·t/2h)), with the ground/tire pivot replaced by a wall/roller pivot.

                (a) Ballast is useless. Adding mass raises overturning and restoring moments in identical proportion. Weight is not a stability lever.

                (b) Sensitivity is inverse-square-root in the height difference, so small changes in roller height have large effects:

                h_gh_rh_g − h_rv_crit
                18 mm10 mm8 mm4.95 m/s
                18 mm14 mm4 mm7.00 m/s
                18 mm16 mm2 mm9.90 m/s
                18 mm≥18 mm≤0no limit

                (c) The sign flips at h_r = h_g. Above that, the wall reaction becomes a restoring moment — the barrier presses the car down rather than levering it up. This is the highest-value setup change available and it costs nothing but shims. Practical bound in the other direction: if h_r goes too low the roller can slip under the barrier lip entirely, which is a separate and worse failure. The window is bounded at both ends.

                Two limits on this formula, both from Gillespie's primary text (§10.4). First, the rigid-body form overpredicts — compliance in the chassis, plates, roller mounts and tires all reduce the real threshold, never raise it. Second, corner entry is a step input with essentially zero roll damping, and roll overshoot lowers the threshold further still. A computed v_crit is therefore a ceiling, not a prediction. Measure it (§8.2) rather than trusting the number.

                4.4 Roller cant

                F_down = N_w · tan θ

                N_w is large — at 14 m/s on a 0.4 m radius, N_w ≈ 64 N, about 50× the car's weight. Even small angles produce substantial downforce:

                This is the only meaningful source of downforce available to a Mini 4WD. It comes from the barrier, not the air.

                5. Traction limit and corner exit

                5.1 The tire saturates long before the motor

                F_grip  = μ · m · g                        ≈ 0.77 N
F_tract = η_d · G · Kt · I / R_w  (I ≈ 55 A) ≈ 45 N

                Ratio: 45–90× depending on μ. The wheels spin; the motor is never the constraint. A stock Tamiya Type 130 delivers roughly 0.7–1.2 N at the tire, which is approximately the traction limit — Tamiya sized it correctly.

                5.2 Acceleration

                m · dv/dt = μ · m · g   →   dv/dt = μ · g ≈ 5.9 m/s²

                Mass cancels again. Ballast does not improve acceleration, and neither does torque.

                5.3 Wall-assisted acceleration

                m·dv/dt = μ·m·g + N_w(μ·tanθ − μ_r) − F_aero

Net accelerator when:   μ · tanθ > μ_r   →   tanθ > μ_r / μ

                With μ_r = 0.03 and μ = 0.6: tanθ > 0.05, so θ > 2.9°. Below ~3° the barrier costs more in roller drag than it returns in traction. This is where the ≥3° cant specification comes from.

                5.4 The transient loophole, closed

                Does a low-turn motor at least reach the traction ceiling faster? Check the electrical time constant τ_e = L / R_total. For a 130-size armature L is on the order of tens of µH against R_total ≈ 0.084 Ω, giving τ_e well under a millisecond. Corner-exit events run 50–300 ms — two to three orders of magnitude longer. Both motors hit their ceiling essentially instantly, and both ceilings are clipped to the same μmg.

                6. Flight and landing

                6.1 Attitude in the air is fixed at takeoff

                Gravity acts at the CoG, so there is zero external torque in flight. Angular momentum about the CoG is conserved. You cannot rotate the car in the air; you can only control its rotation rate at takeoff and what happens on impact.

                6.2 Wheel spin-up drives chassis pitch

                J_w · Δω_w + J_c · Δω_c = 0        Δφ = (J_w / J_c) · Δω_w · T

J_w ≈ 1.3×10⁻⁶ kg·m²    J_c ≈ 2.4×10⁻⁴ kg·m²    ratio ≈ 1/190 to 1/215

                For a 0.3 s flight:

                MotorΔω_wΔφ
                Kv 6,650 (Scenario C wind)6,000 rpm~50° nose-up
                Kv 2,850 (Scenario A/B wind)2,250 rpm~21° nose-up

                Fifty degrees of nose-up lands the car on its rear bumper. This is why light wheels and hollow axles are a top-priority purchase — J_w is the only term in the numerator you can reduce. Absolute angles are order-of-magnitude; the ranking between the two motors is robust, since it depends only on the ratio of their Δω_w.

                6.3 Mass damper as momentum cancellation

                On impact the chassis rebounds upward at u = e · v_imp, while the damper mass — decoupled by its free travel — is still descending at v_imp:

                p = m_c · u − m_d · v_imp = 0   →   m_d ≈ e · m_c   →   20–30 g

                Which is what commercial damper sets weigh. Not a coincidence.

                6.4 Damper timing

                t_delay ≈ s / v_imp        v_imp = √(2·g·h)
s = v_imp · t_impact ≈ 2–6 mm    →    practical range 2–4 mm

                Too tight and the damper acts during impact and does nothing; too loose and the car has already left the ground.

                7. Design synthesis — working back from v_crit

                7.1 The logic

                1. v_crit caps usable speed, and it contains no motor term.
                2. v_term for a high-Kv motor is 3–4× v_crit. The excess is not wasted — it launches the car off at the first corner.
                3. Therefore: choose Kv and gearing so the motor's own no-load speed sits below v_crit, making back-EMF a passive governor.

                7.2 Solving for Kv

                Ke = V · R_w / (v_nl · G)          Kv = 9.5493 / Ke

v_nl,target = 0.85 to 0.90 × v_crit,measured

                Set the target deliberately, not by gear-table accident. v_crit is inverse-square-root sensitive to (h_g − h_r) — a 6 mm shift moves it from 4.95 to 9.90 m/s — and the geometry inputs are estimates until measured. Worked example for car A at a measured v_crit of 10.5 m/s, 87.5% target, G = 3.5, R_w = 0.015:

                Ke = 7.4 × 0.015 / (9.2 × 3.5) = 3.45×10⁻³
Kv = 9.5493 / 3.45×10⁻³ ≈ 2,770 RPM/V

                7.3 Turn count — derived for A, inherited by B

                Kv = Kv_baseline · (N_baseline / N) · (1 / Φ)

                With a 5,700 RPM/V baseline at 7 turns on ferrite and Φ = 2.0 for N52, a target near 2,850 RPM/V returns N = 7 for car A.

                N = 7 is derived for A and inherited by B — do not read it as a coincidence. Scenario A's Kv follows from A's measured v_crit. Scenario B then reuses A's motor, with B's gearing chosen afterward to suit it. The two produce identical Ke because B's drivetrain was fitted to the winding, not the reverse. Treat N = 7 for B as a hypothesis to verify (procedure below). It also depends on the ferrite baseline being exactly 2× the target and Φ being exactly 2.0 — both scaffolding figures.

                Verification procedure for car B:

                1. Measure v_crit at B's tightest corner independently. Expect it lower than A's, since v_crit ∝ √r — if B's tightest radius is 0.25 m against A's 0.40 m, B's ceiling is ~79% of A's.
                2. Compute B's inherited no-load speed: 7.4 × 0.013 / (3.35e-3 × 4) = 7.18 m/s.
                3. Test: is v_nl ≤ 0.90 × v_crit,B? That requires v_crit,B ≥ 8.0 m/s.
                4. If yes — the shared motor is fine; keep it.
                5. If no — shorten the gearing first (4.2 → 6.84 m/s, 5:1 → 5.74 m/s). Gearing is free; rewinding is not.
                6. If no ratio is short enough — wind B separately: N_B = N_A × (Ke_B / Ke_A).

                7.5 Back-EMF as a speed governor

                This is the payoff of the low-Kv choice, and it is larger than the safety-margin argument suggests. Above v_nl the tractive force goes negative: the motor becomes a brake with restoring force c·(v − v_nl). For the revised 7-turn motor:

                c = η_d · G² · Kt · Ke / (R_w² · R_total) ≈ 5.8 N per m/s

                against 0.95 N per m/s for the Kv 6,650 Scenario C wind. Six times stiffer, because Kt is higher and R_total lower.

                Gravity cannot outrun it. On a descent of angle α, steady-state overspeed above v_nl is bounded by Δv ≤ m·g·sin α / c:

                Descent αΔv, Scenario A/B windΔv, Scenario C wind
                10°0.04 m/s0.23 m/s
                20°0.08 m/s0.46 m/s
                30°0.11 m/s0.67 m/s

                These are upper bounds — aero and wall drag add further braking. Two consequences: the car cannot meaningfully overspeed v_nl under gravity, so a downhill run-in to a corner does not defeat the design; and less braking hardware is needed, since brake sponges only have to manage landing attitude rather than bleed corner-entry speed.

                Battery sag fails safe. Cruise current is ~11 A into a pack of ~50 mΩ, so terminal voltage sags roughly 0.55 V and falls further as the pack depletes. Since v_nl ∝ V and v_crit contains no V term:

                v_nl(6.85 V) = 9.5 × (6.85 / 7.4) ≈ 8.8 m/s      (−7%)

                The margin against v_crit grows through a run, never shrinks — and 7% is roughly the deliberate margin §7.2 asks for anyway.

                7.6 Why the motor stops being a grenade

                P_cu = I² · R_winding = 11² × (0.013 to 0.038) ≈ 1.6 to 4.6 W

Earlier stall-optimised 14-turn design: 54 A through 72 mΩ = 210 W

                Two orders of magnitude. Nothing in the revised motor is thermally or mechanically stressed, so nothing self-destructs. The grenade premise dissolves once gearing is matched to what the chassis can physically hold.

                Exception: on a straight-line drag layout there are no corners, v_crit does not exist as a constraint, and the original high-Kv low-turn design is correct unmodified.

                8. What to measure, and how

                Every estimate can be replaced with a measurement. In rough order of value:

                1. h_g — tilt test. Tilt the car on a board until it topples. h_g = (t / 2) / tan(θ_tip). Needs a digital angle gauge or a phone inclinometer app.
                2. v_crit — slow-motion video, at the tightest corner. Find the speed at which the inside wheels first lift, by counting frames across a known section length. Use the tightest corner, not a representative one: v_nl is a single layout-wide motor property, so it must respect the worst case. Since v_crit ∝ √r, a corner 40% tighter has a ceiling 22% lower. This is the single most valuable number here, and it costs nothing.
                3. Cd·A and Crr — coast-down test. Release at known speed with the motor disconnected, record velocity decay, and fit m·dv/dt = −½ρ·Cd·A·v² − Crr·m·g. Ten minutes replaces two estimates.
                4. μ — inclined plane. Tilt a section of track surface until a non-rolling car slides: μ = tan(θ_slide).
                5. Kv — tachometer. Kv = RPM_noload / V. A large discrepancy means Φ = 2.0 is wrong for the magnets you actually bought. Marketplace grade claims are not verifiable and counterfeit neodymium is common, so this is the one place the whole gearing chain can silently break. Read the no-load current on a multimeter at the same time, with the car on a stand so the gearbox turns too: it sets how long a pack lasts at cruise and how fast the car coasts down once the power goes (§11.7).
                6. R_fixed and R_winding — optional. These govern cruise current, heating, and governor stiffness — but not turn count, since neither v_crit nor v_nl contains any resistance term.
                7. Scenario C only — the μ the tires have while sliding, and the sign of body lift. Every drag run is a burnout (§11.7), so the μ that sets the run is the sliding one, and the inclined plane in item 4 measures the static one. Film the first 1–2 m of a launch at 240 fps against a ruler; aero is negligible that early, so x = ½·a₀·t² gives a₀ and μ = a₀/g + Crr directly. For lift, a crude sign check: stand the car on a kitchen scale, block it from rolling, and aim a leaf blower at its nose at roughly strip speed (a cheap anemometer confirms it). A reading that falls means the body lifts. It gives no usable magnitude — the floor under the car is not moving the way the strip does — but a body that lifts on the scale should not go down the strip unfixed (§11.6(b)).

                10.4 Cross-check against Gillespie's rollover treatment — verified against the primary text

                §4.3's threshold has been checked against Gillespie's Chapter 9 directly. The formula is confirmed exactly, and the primary text carries four things no secondary summary of it does.

                The confirmation

                Gillespie takes moments about the center of contact for the outside tires — the same pivot choice as §4.2, just at ground level — and writes his Equation 9.1 as ΣM_o = 0 = M·a_y·h − M·g·φ·h + F_zi·t − M·g·t/2, where φ is road cross-slope. Setting the inside-wheel load F_zi to zero gives his Equation 9.4, a_y/g = (t/2 + φh)/h, and he states plainly that with no cross-slope the rollover threshold is simply "t over 2h." Substituting a_y = v²/r:

                Gillespie Eq. 9.4 (φ = 0), expressed as a speed:
    v_crit = √( g · r · (t/2) / h )

This document, §4.3:
    v_crit = √( g · r ·  b   / (h_g − h_r) )

Set h_r → 0 and b ↔ t/2, h_g ↔ h  →  identical.

                So §4's formula is a strict generalization of Gillespie's rigid-vehicle case, recovered exactly at ground-level pivot. The derivation route differs slightly — he solves the general moment balance and then sets F_zi = 0, while §4.2 equates overturning and restoring moments directly — but the two are algebraically the same operation.

                (a) Wheel lift-off is the correct threshold definition

                Gillespie defines the limit condition as the moment the inside wheels' load reaches zero, and argues explicitly that wheel lift-off — not tip-over — should be treated as the beginning of rollover, because the vehicle is inherently unstable beyond that point: any further roll reduces the equilibrium lateral acceleration, so the excess drives the angle further away from equilibrium.

                §8.2 already instructs you to find "the speed at which the inside wheels first lift." That was chosen because it is the visible event on slow-motion video. It turns out to be the textbook-correct criterion as well, for a reason worth knowing: past inside-wheel lift, recovery is not a matter of degree — the geometry actively drives the car further over.

                (b) The formula overpredicts — a caveat this document was missing

                Gillespie is direct about this: the rigid-vehicle estimates are very conservative, predicting thresholds greater than the actual, and are more useful for comparing vehicles than for predicting absolute performance. He identifies two compliance mechanisms, each costing roughly 5% for a passenger car:

                Mechanism (Gillespie)EffectMini 4WD analogue
                Body roll about the suspension roll center shifts CG laterally, shortening the restoring arm~5%Mostly absent — no suspension. Except MS chassis, which has genuine inner suspension and does have a roll centre.
                Lateral tire deflection moves the contact patch inboard, effectively reducing track width~5%Partially transfers: chassis and FRP-plate flex, roller-mount deflection, and tire squirm all reduce effective b under load.

                Both mechanisms reduce the threshold, never raise it. So a computed v_crit — including the one this page's calculator produces — is an optimistic ceiling, not a prediction. This is a direct argument for the measurement-first workflow in §8: a measured v_crit has all of this already baked in.

                (c) Corner entry is a step input, and roll damping is near zero

                This is the most consequential thing the primary text adds, and it is genuinely new physics for this document rather than a caveat.

                Gillespie shows that under a suddenly applied lateral acceleration the roll angle responds as a second-order system and overshoots the steady-state value, so wheel lift-off can occur at lower lateral acceleration than the quasi-static analysis predicts. His figures: for an automobile or SUV, a step-steer input reduces the rollover threshold by about 30% below the "t over 2h" value, against only about 10% for the quasi-static suspended case. Critically, he also shows the lowest threshold occurs at zero roll damping, rising with damping ratio at a diminishing rate.

                Both conditions describe a Mini 4WD entering a corner almost exactly:

                • The input is a step, not a ramp. A road car's lateral acceleration builds as the driver winds on steering. A Mini 4WD meets the barrier and goes from zero lateral acceleration to v²/r in the length of the transition piece. There is no gradual application available.
                • Roll damping is essentially zero — no shock absorbers, no bushings, nothing dissipative in the roll plane. Gillespie's worst case is the default condition.

                The caveat on transferring this: his overshoot mechanism needs a roll degree of freedom with stiffness and inertia, which a road car gets from its suspension. A rigid Mini 4WD chassis has no suspension, but it is not actually rigid — chassis flex, FRP plate compliance, roller-mount deflection and tire squirm supply an effective roll stiffness. So the mechanism transfers; the magnitude does not. Do not apply a 30% derate — that is a car-and-SUV number. Treat it as the reason a measured v_crit will land below the computed one, and as a second, previously unnoticed job for the mass dampers of §6.3: they are the only meaningful source of roll damping on the car.

                (d) Without the barrier, a Mini 4WD cannot roll over at all

                Gillespie notes that for passenger cars the lateral acceleration needed to reach rollover exceeds what the tires can generate, so the car spins out on a flat surface rather than rolling. Running the same comparison for a Mini 4WD makes the point far more starkly:

                VehicleCG heightTrack widthRollover threshold t/2h
                Heavy truck (Gillespie)60–85 in70–72 in0.4–0.6 g
                Passenger van (Gillespie)30–40 in65–70 in0.8–1.1 g
                Sports car (Gillespie)18–20 in50–60 in1.2–1.7 g

                At 2.08 g against a tire friction coefficient of roughly 0.6, the car slides out roughly three and a half times before it would ever roll. The barrier is not merely the pivot — it is the entire reason rollover is possible. A Mini 4WD on an open flat surface cannot tip itself over by cornering; only the wall, by raising the pivot to h_r and supplying an unlimited lateral reaction, creates the failure mode this document spends §4 and §7 designing around.

                And the accelerations involved are in a different regime entirely. At h_g − h_r = 4 mm and b = 50 mm, the effective threshold is b/(h_g − h_r) = 12.5 g — roughly seven times a sports car's — which is why a 7 m/s Mini 4WD is a genuinely extreme cornering machine despite being slower than a bicycle.

                Smaller items
                • One model in the chapter does use a raised pivot. The quasi-static and transient models all pivot about the vehicle's own tire contact patch, but not the tripped-rollover treatment, where a vehicle skids into a curb and pivots about the wheel/curb contact — a raised, external pivot, structurally the closest analogue in the chapter to the wall-roller case here. He models it energetically (rotational kinetic plus potential energy versus the energy needed to lift the CG over the outside wheels) rather than as a closed-form threshold, so there is still no published formula to compare §4.3 against directly.
                • Independent confirmation that mass cancels. Gillespie's sensitivity study on the tripped-rollover model found vehicle weight has little effect on rollover propensity except indirectly through ride height — an empirical echo of the analytic cancellation in §4.3(a), arrived at by a completely different route.
                • Banked sections. His cross-slope term carries over directly. For a banked corner of angle φ, the numerator gains a term: v_crit = √( g·r·(b + φ·(h_g − h_r)) / (h_g − h_r) ) under the same small-angle assumption. Banking raises the ceiling. The calculator does not model this; treat banked sections as a bonus, not a design input.
                • The tilt test survives, for a different reason than it might appear. Gillespie warns that tilt-table testing is invalidated above about 45° because reduced normal loading lets the suspension extend and raise the body, causing premature rollover — and a Mini 4WD tips at around 64°, well past that. The objection does not apply here: the mechanism is suspension extension, and a rigid chassis has none. More importantly, §8.1 uses the tilt test to measure CG height, a static geometric property, not to measure the rollover threshold itself. Different measurement, different validity conditions.

                11.3 Mass reverses sign — the one place ballast can help

                Every other section of this document concludes that mass cancels. On a drag strip it does not, and the reason is worth stating precisely:

                m · dv/dt = μ·m·g − ½ρ·Cd·A·v² − Crr·m·g

dv/dt = g(μ − Crr) − (½ρ·Cd·A·v²) / m
                      └─────────────┘
                      does NOT scale with m

                Grip scales with mass; aerodynamic drag does not. So the drag term's penalty is divided by mass, and a heavier car suffers less from it. At 15 m/s with a 130 g car, aero costs about 2.4 m/s² out of 5.9 — over 40% of available acceleration. At 200 g the same aero costs only 1.6 m/s².

                This is the exact inverse of §4.3(a). It holds only because there is no corner to pay for the mass in — and only if μ does not fall as the load rises, which is the assumption the equation above hides in writing μ·m·g.

                Rubber does not obey that assumption, and ballast has to be tested, not assumed. A rubber tire's friction coefficient generally falls as the load on it rises — car-tire data commonly show something like a tenth of μ lost per doubling of load, and a smooth elastic contact in the Hertzian limit goes as μ ∝ N^−⅓. Write it as μ ∝ m^−n and integrate: +50 g on a 130 g car at 30 m is a net gain only while n is below 0.087 with the stock body, or 0.058 with the clean body of §11.6 — i.e. only if the extra 50 g costs the tires less than about 2–3% of their μ. At n = 0.1 it is already 6–20 ms slower; at 0.2, 54–67 ms slower. Nobody has measured n for a Mini 4WD tire, so the honest rule is: time the strip with and without the ballast, and keep whichever is faster. An earlier version of this section said "more is monotonically better until something breaks"; that holds only at n = 0.

                11.4 Consumable, not grenade

                The original brief called for self-destruction at the finish line after 15–25 seconds. A drag run is 2–3 seconds. Very little destroys a motor in 3 seconds, and the thermal case is unambiguous. An earlier version of this paragraph put the launch current spike at roughly 78 A through 4 mΩ for perhaps 100 ms — 2.4 J into a gram of copper, about 6 K. The spike is real but far shorter than that: the tires break loose within about 0.1 ms of switch-on (§11.7), so the only load the spike accelerates is the wheels and armature, not the car. The current falls back to the ~6 A burnout level on the drivetrain's mechanical time constant — a few milliseconds — putting roughly 0.1–0.2 J into the winding, well under 1 K. A hand-release start, wheels already spinning, has no spike at all. Nothing melts, by a wider margin than first claimed.

                What a low-turn motor genuinely does suffer on a drag strip is mechanical wear at extreme RPM: brush bounce and arcing at high commutation frequency, commutator erosion, bearing load. That accumulates over tens of runs, not within one. The right framing is a consumable motor — rebuild the brushes every meeting, expect the commutator to need dressing — rather than a grenade timed to the finish line. The tires join the brushes on that list: every run slides them for its whole length (§11.7).

                Where a drag car can genuinely overheat something is after the finish. A catch that jams the wheels stalls the motor at V/R_total ≈ 80–110 A, and what cooks is not the motor but the 2S pack feeding it (§11.8).

                On venue. The Grand Podium in Semarang publicly describes its layout as a 130 m+ Japan Style endurance circuit for a Technical League, stating plainly that it is not a sprint format. Their listing does say "All Class", so a straight-line class may run there occasionally, but a dedicated drag strip is not something their own material advertises — ask before building for it. This section applies wherever you find a genuine straight-line class, not specifically there.

The ties assume μ does not depend on how fast the tire slides — and every wind in the table slides its tires from launch (§11.7), a 3-turn at more than twice the sliding speed of a 7-turn. If Mini 4WD rubber loses grip as sliding speed rises, as rubber generally does, the 7-turn is not tied but slightly quicker, which only strengthens this section's advice. Nothing here has measured it; §8.7's launch film settles it.

                11.6 Scenario C at the physical limit

                §11.2 sized the motor so that it stays out of the way. This section asks what limits the trap speed once it has, how much of that can be removed, and where physics — rather than a part you can buy — finally stops the car getting faster.

The FAQ's advice not to optimise body shape for drag is right on a cornered layout, where aero is ~3% of the loss budget at 14 m/s.                 (b) The sign of the body's lift matters as much as its drag. A curved body a few millimetres off the ground can make lift, and lift takes normal force off the tires exactly as downforce adds it. In the closed form both appear as an effective drag area, Cd·A − (μ − Crr)·C_z·A, so a unit of lift costs 0.87 of what a unit of drag costs. At 30 m a C_z·A of −0.001 m² — only 0.23 N of lift at 20 m/s — erases the whole clean-body gain (2.798 s, against the stock body's 2.797 s), and −0.002 makes the car slower than stock (2.851 s). Nothing here has measured either sign for any Mini 4WD body; §8.7 has a crude check. This is Scenario C's version of the h_g/h_r transposition in §4: the calculator cannot tell you the sign, and the wrong one is silent.

                (c) Ballast is conditional. It is worth 26 ms at 30 m on top of the clean body — if the tires keep their μ under the extra 50 g. §11.3 has the arithmetic: with the clean body it pays only if the extra load costs the tires less than about 2% of their μ, which rubber is not known to manage. Time it both ways.

                (d) A wing (outlaw). The row assumes a wing of about 90 × 30 mm making C_z·A = 0.0015 m² of downforce (an effective lift coefficient around 0.55) for 0.0004 m² of extra drag — a lift-to-drag ratio of about 3.75. Both are guesses at a Reynolds number near 40,000, a few centimetres off the ground, in the wake of a bluff body. If they hold it is worth 39 ms at 30 m. Because downforce enters multiplied by (μ − Crr), a wing earns its place only while its own lift-to-drag ratio stays above 1/(μ − Crr) ≈ 1.15 — easy on paper, unknown in that wake.

                (e) A suction fan (outlaw) — larger than every other row combined. Everything above acts through λ or μ. A fan raises the normal force at zero speed, so it lifts the launch acceleration itself: a₀ = (μ − Crr)·(g + F_s/m). One newton on a 195 g car is about 120 Pa of depression under a floor of about 0.0084 m²; leaking out through a 1 mm skirt gap, that is about 0.65 W of air power, a few watts electrical. It is worth 510 ms and 5.2 m/s at 30 m over the wing row. The objections are all engineering, not physics: every track-panel joint breaks the seal, ram air at 20+ m/s pushes into the front gap, it is a second motor on the pack, and no class this page knows of permits it. It is here because the question was what physics allows, and this is the answer to it: the μg ceiling is a ceiling on weight, not on normal force.

                (f) Slip control (outlaw). Every C run is a burnout (§11.7). Rubber usually grips harder at a small slip than sliding flat out, so a current-limited drive that holds the tires near peak slip would raise the working μ. If the peak-to-sliding ratio of a Mini 4WD tire is 1.2 — plausible for rubber, unmeasured here — it is worth another 203 ms at 30 m. It needs electronics, and it is the least certain row in the table.

                Every run is a burnout — and what that costs

                From about 0.1 ms until the car catches its own wheels, the tires slide. The wheels run where motor force equals grip, at a rim speed of (F₀ − μmg)/c — about 95–97% of v_nl — while the car climbs from zero toward it. On a correctly geared car the two meet at about the finish line; with surplus Kv they never meet. Three consequences:

                • The μ in every formula here is the sliding μ, which is why §8.7 measures it from a launch rather than on an inclined plane.
                • Half the motor's work goes into the tire contact. Tuned car, 30 m: 43 J burnt in the contact against 40 J of kinetic energy at the line. Tires are consumables in Scenario C, like brushes — check their diameter between rounds, because a worn tire also lowers v_nl.
                • Surplus Kv is not free. At 15 m the tuned car runs 1.899 s on a 3-turn wind and 1.899 s on a 4-turn — but the 3-turn burns 39 J in the tires against 24 J, and spins at about 48,000 rpm against 36,000. Same time, 60% more tire heat and a third more brush speed. Fit the matched wind, not the grenade.

                The last point has a sharper version. The ties above and in §11.5 assume μ does not depend on how fast the tire slides. Rubber's sliding friction generally falls as sliding speed rises, and a grenade slides its tires faster than a matched wind for the whole run. If Mini 4WD rubber behaves that way, the matched wind is not tied — it is quicker. Filming both winds' launches on the same tires (§8.7) settles it in an afternoon.

                11.8 Stopping the car at the end

                The tuned car crosses a 30 m line at 21.0 m/s (75.6 km/h) carrying 40 J. Stopping it is exactly the problem of catching it after a fall from v²/2g = 22.5 m — about seven storeys — and it has to be solved at every finish, by the track rather than by the car.

                What cannot stop it

                • Its own tires. Braking through the tires is capped at μg, exactly as accelerating was. Shorting the motor locks the wheels into a skid that needs 22 m from 21 m/s — as long as the strip. Brake sponges are no better; on a flat strip they do not even touch.
                • Coasting. With the power cut, aero and rolling resistance alone would need 211 m from 21 m/s. The motor stays geared to the wheels, and its own friction shortens that to about 44 m (§11.7) — still far more than any catch box.
                • A wall, or a towel against one. At 20 m/s, 10 mm of crush is an average of 2,000 g — 3,600 N through a 180 g car's nose. 40 mm of padding is still 500 g.
                • A hand. The last metre takes 50 ms, a quarter of a human reaction time, so a catch cannot be timed; a hand simply held in the lane gives 20–50 mm of crush — 400–1,000 g, delivered through roller edges, with the tires still turning at 21 m/s. Do not catch a Scenario C car by hand.

                The catch box, per lane

                1. Straight on. Continue the lane and its walls past the finish sensor, so the car meets the absorber square.
                2. Roof the last 1.5 m or so with clear acrylic or polycarbonate a few millimetres above the car. This is not optional. Taking moments exactly as in §4, a stop at deceleration a tips the car over one axle unless the absorber's push passes within Δh = L·g/a of the CoG height, where L ≈ 40–45 mm is the distance from that axle to the CoG. At 30 g that is ±1.5 mm — not a tolerance anyone can hold. Front rollers at CoG height, which §4 asks for anyway, keep the moment small; the roof is what makes it safe.
                3. A lane-filling column of open-cell foam, at least the stroke ÷ 0.75 long, because foam bottoms out at about 70–80% compression: about 1 m for the tuned car at 30 m, 1.4 m at 25 m/s. Viscoelastic ("memory") foam rebounds least. Face it with a thin smooth skin — a 2 mm EVA sheet — so rollers cannot tear crumbs loose, and cut it into 10–15 cm slabs so a damaged slab is replaced rather than the column.
                4. A hard floor under where the car ends up — an aluminium plate over the track panel. The car will sit there with its wheels turning.

                Two hazards that have nothing to do with breakage

                A jammed drivetrain is a stalled motor. Anything that can wind into an axle — netting, loose fabric, foam crumbs — stops the wheels, and a stalled 3-turn wind draws V/R_total ≈ 80–110 A: 170–240 C from a 450 mAh pack, heating it through its own ~50 mΩ at roughly 10–20 K per second. The car is at the far end of the strip, twenty seconds' walk from the start. That rules out nets, and any catch that lets the wheels leave the hard floor for something soft.

                A free drivetrain is a stationary burnout. Pressed into the foam with the power on, the car slides its tires at about 21 m/s under μmg: 24–33 W into four contact patches and the track under them, until someone arrives. Cut the power as soon as the car stops — a marshal at the catch, or, where class rules allow electronics, a latching cut-off tripped by a magnet under the lane after the finish.

                Calibrate it at low speed first

                Foam grades vary too much, and stiffen too much with impact speed, to specify from a shop label. Measure instead: run a slower car into the catch — the Scenario A car (7 turns, 3.5:1, 30 mm wheels) reaches 9.4 m/s on a 30 m strip — film it, and read the stroke s₁ off marks on the lane wall. Stroke scales with the square of speed, so the full-speed stroke is s₁·(v/v₁)²: at 21 m/s, (21/9.4)² ≈ 5×. If s₁ is 0.12 m, the column needs about 0.6 m of stroke. Size for 20 g on that prediction, not 30: foam stiffens as the impact gets faster, so the real stop will be shorter and harder than the low-speed test says.

                When there is no room for a metre of foam

                Stroke goes as v², so the one large lever left is speed: gear for a lower trap speed at venues with a short run-off — 30% slower halves the stroke needed. Past that, choose what breaks. Make the front roller stay the fuse — fixed so it shears or pops off before the chassis cracks, and cheap to replace. Keep the LiPo clear of anything that can puncture it, and retire any pack that comes back dented or puffy. Re-check Kv after a hard stop (§8.5): N52 magnets are brittle, and a cracked one can show up as a higher Kv.

                The contactless option, for a permanent strip

                An aluminium fin under the car running between rows of magnets brakes by eddy currents, with a force proportional to speed, F = k·v. Its useful property is that speed then falls linearly with distance, v(x) = v₀ − (k/m)·x, with the peak deceleration at entry. At k ≈ 1 N per m/s — an order-of-magnitude figure for a 1 mm fin in a gap of a few tenths of a tesla, not a design — a 180 g car arriving at 20 m/s peaks at 11 g and is down to a crawl within roughly 3–4 m, after which any pad will do. With the power still on it crawls at the speed where k·v balances the tires' push, μmg/k ≈ 1.6 m/s. Nothing touches the car. It needs a fin on every car and metres of magnet array, and it is included as what roller coasters do, not as something built here.

                Sizing the fan

                The suction force is the pressure drop times the sealed area, and the fan has to replace whatever leaks out under the skirt:

                Δp        = F_s / A_sealed            = 1 N / 0.0122 m²              ≈ 82 Pa  (8.4 mmH₂O)
Q         = C_d · P · h · √(2·Δp/ρ)   = 0.6 · 0.48 m · 1 mm · 12 m/s ≈ 3.5 L/s (7.3 CFM)
air power = Q · Δp                                                    ≈ 0.28 W

                So the blower has to move about 7 CFM against about 8 mmH₂O. Read both off its pressure–flow curve rather than its headline airflow, which is quoted at zero pressure, and budget a few watts from the pack — a small blower wastes most of its input. Two things move the requirement. The skirt gap: leak flow is proportional to it, so 0.5 mm halves it, at the cost of catching on panel joints. And speed: ram air forces its way under the front skirt, so holding the same 1 N takes about 4.5 L/s at 30 m/s. The model holds F_s constant; a blower that falls short at speed gives less at the end of the run.

                Measure the suction first

                Every E figure rests on that 1 N, and a kitchen scale under the car cannot see it: the chamber pulls the car down and the scale's platform up by the same amount, and the two cancel. Measure it the other way round. Stand the car on a track panel fixed to the bench, hook a digital hanging scale — 1 g resolution, peak hold — to a loop over the CoG, start the blower and lift slowly. The peak just before the car lets go, minus its weight, is F_s; about 100 g on the scale is 1 N. At 30 m the first 0.5 N is worth 367 ms and the next 0.5 N another 249 ms, so the build order is body and skirt, then blower, measured at every step, and the wing last, as the smallest term.

                Stage 2 — what physics allows once the rulebook is gone

                Stage 1 kept three things a rulebook would have asked for: the brushed motor, the 1 mm skirt gap and Tamiya's envelope. Stage 2 drops all three, and the first consequence is that the car changes regime. With enough suction the tires can push harder than the motor can follow, so the binding limit moves from grip to power, and the levers reorder themselves. In order of payoff, at 30 m:

                1. Contact skirts, for 5 N of suction. With no clearance rule the skirt can touch the track — PTFE-tipped strips or a fine brush — and the leak that set Stage 1's fan budget nearly disappears. 5 N over the same 122 cm² is about 410 Pa (42 mmH₂O): a job for a high-pressure blower, rated in tens of mmH₂O rather than in CFM. Skirt friction is a few hundredths of a newton against 6 N of drive; the track-panel joints, which a rigid skirt would strike, are why the tips are soft or the skirt is a brush. This is the lever the rest of the list exists to serve.
                2. A brushless motor and an ESC, because the car is now power-limited. At the 30 m line the 5 N car needs about 250 W at the wheels. Held to the brushed 3-turn wind's 49,000 rpm wall it runs 1.616 s instead of 1.427. A brushless inrunner has no commutator to wear or bounce, so 100,000 rpm becomes a question for its bearings and rotor rather than its brushes. Budget about 300 W at the wheel — 375 W at the shaft — for two seconds, from a 3S pack that can deliver it; 150 W costs 17 ms, and 600 W buys nothing at 5 N.
                3. Gear for power: 44 mm wheels, 100,000 rpm, a taller ratio — pick two. All three set the same ceiling, v_nl = 2π·R_w·rpm/(60·G). For a power-limited car the best ceiling is about 1.5 times the trap speed, not §11.2's 1.05, because the motor should be near its peak-power speed at the end of the run. 100,000 rpm on 44 mm wheels lands there on the stock 3.5:1 (65.8 m/s); so does 100,000 rpm on 35 mm wheels at 2.8:1 (65.4 m/s). All three together give 82.3 m/s — no slower at 30 m, but an uncontrolled burnout would then spin the tires to 54 m/s.
                4. Slip control, now mandatory rather than a bonus. Geared for power, the motor would spin the tires to about 48 m/s at launch if nothing stopped it. The ESC's current limit holds them near peak slip instead. If rubber's peak-to-sliding grip ratio is the 1.2 that §11.6 assumed, this is also worth 133 ms at 30 m.
                5. Belted tires. A spinning tread pulls itself outward with a hoop stress ρ·v²: 1.9 MPa at 41 m/s, the same order as soft rubber's stiffness, so an unbelted tire grows visibly and narrows its own contact patch. The fix RC speed-runners use is a belt — fibre wound under the tread, or tape over it — on wheels balanced for about 20,000 rpm.
                6. Metal gears. About 6 N at a 22 mm radius is 0.13 N·m at the wheels, several times anything the plastic gear sets were sized for.
                7. Lightness. With the fan's share divided by mass, ±10 g is ∓34 ms.
                8. A long, low-drag body, and the wing. With no length rule the tail can taper; together they are worth 10–15 ms. Small, because at 41 m/s drag is 1.6 N against 6 N of drive.
                9. Thrust that bypasses the tires. A ducted fan pushing 2.5 N at a standstill, fading to nothing at its 45 m/s exhaust speed, is worth 104 ms for another 30 g and a couple of hundred watts. Stored-gas and pyrotechnic thrust go further — school CO₂ dragsters cover 20 m in about a second, on a guide wire — but they make the car a pressure vessel or a fire source among spectators, and this page does not design them.

                1. Bahan motor

                Kelas termal (thermal class) kawat: Class 155 (F) sudah cukup — rugi tembaga (copper loss) keadaan tunak cuma ~3 W. Class 200 (AIW/EIW-AI) tetap bagus kalau harganya mirip, tapi bukan lagi syarat. Kebutuhan kawat asli ~1,3 m per motor; 1 ons cukup untuk puluhan percobaan.

                2. Chassis

                Keduanya single-shaft. Jangan MA / MS / ME — itu double-shaft (PRO), susah di-rewind.

                3. Drivetrain

                Kenapa velg ringan & as hollow naik prioritas. Saat mobil melompat, roda berputar bebas dan momentum sudutnya (angular momentum) harus diimbangi — chassis berputar mendongak sebagai reaksi. Ini kekekalan momentum sudut (conservation of angular momentum). Momen inersia (moment of inertia) roda, J_w, adalah satu-satunya suku yang bisa dikecilkan. Ini penyebab utama mobil jungkir di landing, bukan setelan damper.

                Ban soft untuk B. Akselerasi keluar tikungan = μ × g, dengan μ = koefisien gesek (coefficient of friction). Massa tidak berpengaruh, torsi motor tidak berpengaruh — hanya μ, karena mobil dibatasi traksi (traction-limited). Bearing diberi oli.

                4. Setup chassis — bagian paling menentukan

                Aturan tinggi roller. v_crit = √(g·r·b / (h_g − h_r)), dengan h_g = tinggi titik berat (center of gravity), h_r = tinggi kontak roller ke dinding — ini titik tumpu (pivot) tempat momen dihitung — dan b = lengan momen (lever arm) dari dinding ke titik berat. Massa hilang dari persamaan: gaya sentripetal (centripetal force) dan gaya berat naik sebanding sehingga saling meniadakan — menambah pemberat tidak membantu sama sekali. Begitu h_r ≥ h_g, tanda momen gaya (moment of force) berbalik: dinding justru menghasilkan gaya tekan ke bawah (downforce). Ini alasan spacer dan shim ada di urutan atas daftar.

                Sudut roller (cant): minimum 3°. Ambang dari tanθ > μ_r/μ ≈ 0,05. Di bawah ~3°, dinding lebih banyak memperlambat daripada membantu. Pada gaya normal dinding ~60 N, sudut 3° menghasilkan ~3 N gaya tekan ke bawah (downforce) — lebih dari dua kali berat mobil.

                Setelan mass damper (mobil B). Berat total ≈ 20–30 g (dari m_d ≈ e · m_c, dengan e = koefisien restitusi / coefficient of restitution), jarak main bebas 2–4 mm diatur pakai ring shim. Beli set variasi + banyak shim, jangan kit dengan berat mati.

                5. Power

                Spesifikasi pack untuk Skenario A/B longgar. Arus jelajah ~11 A, jauh di bawah arus macet (stall current). Pack 600 mAh 50C (= 30 A) sudah lebih dari cukup. Tetap solder kabel langsung ke motor, bypass terminal pegas.

                6. Alat ukur

                IR tester opsional. Alat ini mengukur hambatan dalam (internal resistance) pack. Kv ditentukan oleh ambang guling v_crit, dan v_crit tidak mengandung R_fixed sama sekali.

                Uji miring (tilt test) untuk h_g: taruh mobil di papan, miringkan pelan sampai terguling. h_g = (t/2) / tan(θ_guling), dengan t = jarak antar ban kiri-kanan.

                Frequently Asked Questions

                Does adding weight make a Mini 4WD corner more stably?
                No. Both the overturning moment and the restoring moment are linear in mass, so mass cancels exactly from v_crit = √(g·r·b/(h_g − h_r)). Ballast changes nothing about when the car climbs the barrier. Roller contact height relative to CoG height is the lever that matters.
                What actually limits a Mini 4WD's top speed on a track with corners?
                The wall-climb threshold, not motor power. A high-Kv motor reaches a terminal velocity of roughly 18–21 m/s, while the geometric rollover ceiling with typical roller heights sits around 5–10 m/s. The excess is not usable — it launches the car off at the first corner.
                Does more motor torque improve acceleration out of a corner?
                No. The tires saturate first. Available grip is about 0.77 N for a 130 g car, while even a modest rewound 130 motor delivers around 45 N at the tire — headroom of 45–90×. Corner-exit acceleration equals μ·g regardless of torque, and mass cancels from that too.
                How high should Mini 4WD rollers be mounted?
                At or above the car's CoG height. When h_r = h_g the overturning moment goes to zero; above it the sign reverses and the barrier presses the car down. The threshold is inverse-square-root sensitive, so a 2 mm shim change can move v_crit by several m/s. Too low is also a failure mode — the roller can slip under the barrier lip.
                Why does my Mini 4WD flip backwards on jumps?
                Angular momentum. In flight there is no external torque about the CoG, so when the unloaded wheels free-spin toward no-load speed, the chassis counter-rotates nose-up. A high-Kv motor can produce roughly 50° of nose-up pitch in a 0.3 s flight versus about 21° for a low-Kv motor. Lighter wheels and hollow axles reduce the coupling.
                How much should a Mini 4WD mass damper weigh?
                About 20–30 g for a 100 g chassis. The damper cancels rebound momentum, so optimal mass is m_d = e · m_c, with e typically 0.2–0.3. Free travel should be 2–4 mm so the damper arrives after the chassis reverses rather than during the impact.
                Does aerodynamic drag matter for a Mini 4WD?
                On a cornered track, barely. At 14 m/s aero drag is about 0.28 N, consuming roughly 4 W against 144 W of available shaft power — around 3% of the loss budget. Roller drag against the barrier is roughly 7× larger and scales identically as v², so there you optimise body shape for CoG height and stability, not for drag coefficient. On a long drag strip the answer reverses: at 20 m/s aero drag takes about half of what the tires can push, and a cleaner body is worth about 50 ms over 30 m — provided it does not make lift, which costs about as much again (§11.6).
                Does a short drag strip need a different motor than a long one?
                No. Below about 10 m every usable motor records the same time — the car is traction-limited at μg for the whole run and never reaches any motor's ceiling. A 3-turn wind and a 7-turn wind tie to three decimal places at 5 m. The crossover is roughly 1.4·v_nl²/(2μg); beyond about 15 m a higher-Kv wind is clearly worth rewinding for. On a short strip, fit the motor you already have and spend the money on softer tires. Ballast is worth a few milliseconds at most there, and only if the tires keep their grip under the load (§11.3).
                Is a self-destructing "grenade" motor worth building?
                Only for a straight-line drag layout. On any track with corners, gearing the motor below the rollover ceiling drops cruise current to around 11 A and copper loss to a few watts, so nothing is stressed enough to fail. A motor that self-destructs is one being asked to produce force the tires cannot transmit and the chassis cannot hold.
                What is the fastest a Mini 4WD can go on a long drag strip?
                Without downforce, no faster at the line than √(2·(μ − Crr)·g·d) — about 22.6 m/s (81 km/h) at 30 m on soft tires, whatever the motor. Ballast and a clean body only close the gap to that ceiling; a tuned car reaches about 93% of it. Only more grip, or a normal force larger than the car's weight — downforce — raises it. Past about 30 m the next limit is motor rpm, so raise the ceiling with larger wheels rather than fewer turns. The top speed arrives at the finish line itself: a correctly geared car is still accelerating when it crosses (§11.6, §11.7).
                Does a suction fan help a Mini 4WD on a drag strip?
                More than anything else, in a class that allows it. A fan is the one change that raises the grip ceiling itself, because it adds normal force without adding mass: a 1 N fan on a 149 g car takes a 30 m run from 2.720 s for the tuned Scenario C car to about 2.07–2.09 s. It also makes ballast counterproductive — with the fan fitted, +50 g costs about 0.1 s at 30 m — and it drives the car into the motor's rpm wall from about 20 m, after which only gearing taller than any stock Tamiya set helps. Scenario E (§11.9) is the full build, and none of it has been built yet.
                With no rules at all, what limits a Mini 4WD drag car?
                Grip first, so suction: with skirts that touch the track, a sealed body can hold about 5 N. Then power — about 300 W at the wheels — which needs a brushless motor near 100,000 rpm, since a brushed motor's commutator is the rpm wall. Then the tires themselves: rubber loses grip under load, and above about 30 m/s the tread's own hoop stress makes unbelted tires balloon, so they need belts and slip control. The model's Stage 2 car runs 30 m in about 1.3–1.4 s at 150–160 km/h. Finally the stop: braking through the tires with the fan still running takes about 22 m from any of these speeds, because the grip that launched the car is the grip that stops it (§11.9).
                How long would a Mini 4WD keep going if the straight never ended?
                A tuned Scenario C car on a 2S 450 mAh pack would top out at about 87 km/h within 4 seconds, then slow gradually as the pack voltage falls — about 4% by 80% charge, 12% by 10% — and fall off quickly below that. The pack would be empty after roughly 5–9 minutes and 7–12 km, depending on how much current the motor and gearbox waste, and the car would stop about 40 m later because the geared-in motor brakes it. The outlaw Stage 2 car tops out near 216 km/h and its ESC cuts off after about a minute and 3.4 km (§11.7).
                How do you stop a Mini 4WD at the end of a long drag strip without breaking it?
                With stroke, not strength. At 21 m/s the car carries the energy of a 22 m fall, and braking through its own tires would need about 22 m — as long as the strip. A roofed, lane-width column of foam about a metre long stops it in roughly 70 ms at about 30 g, which is no more force than its rollers carry in a hard corner. Never a rigid wall, never a net — a jammed drivetrain stalls the motor at around 80–110 A and overheats the LiPo — and never a hand. Cut the power as soon as it stops (§11.8).
